Allocation & the real OOM

chapter 03 · ~14 min read · deep-roots learning program
The 30-second version. The GPU has an MMU — per-process virtual address spaces, page tables, one process-agnostic driver owning the physical HBM — module 01 ch03's machine with three deltas: 2 MiB pages, one owner, peer mappings. cudaMalloc is slow and page-granular, so PyTorch layers a caching allocator on top — module 01 ch19's segregated-fit, replayed. Hence the real OOM: "out of memory" almost never means out of bytes; it means out of contiguous, policy-satisfiable memory — free bytes fenced into holes by live tensors. Fix ladder: steady tensor shapes, expandable_segments, and never-as-a-defragmenter empty_cache().

The GPU has an MMU: ch03's machine, three deltas

Module 01 ch03 built the machine — per-process address spaces, page tables, TLB, demand paging. The GPU runs it unchanged except three deltas (Ampere/Hopper, CUDA 12.x):

  1. Pages are 64 KiB and 2 MiB (CPU: 4 KiB) — same reason as huge pages: one TLB entry covers 2 MiB, not 512 4 KiB pages. A 64-byte tensor never buys a 64-byte mapping; the smallest currency unit is a page.
  2. One owner. Physical HBM belongs to the kernel driver; every process's page tables point into one process-agnostic pool. Per-process accounting is the driver's bookkeeping (what nvidia-smi prints), not yours to compute.
  3. Peer mappings. A page-table entry can point off-device: with NVLink, GPU 0 maps GPU 1's HBM (cudaDeviceEnablePeerAccess) — the walk crosses the wire, a preview of ch04.
 process A VA (49-bit, Ampere/Hopper)      GPU page tables (walked by the GMMU)
 [0x7f0a00000000, +16 MiB) ---->  PTE -> 2 MiB page ----> physical HBM page 0x41
                                  PTE -> 2 MiB page ----> physical HBM page 0x7c
                                  PTE -> 2 MiB page ----> PEER: GPU 1 HBM (NVLink)
 process B VA (49-bit)   ...its own tables; same physical pool, one driver owner

The driving asymmetry: the virtual side is 48-bit-plus (hundreds of TiB); the physical side is a 40–80 GiB island. VA is not scarce. Physical HBM is.

cudaMalloc: one expensive, page-granular call

cudaMalloc(n) returns a VA range backed by physical pages at 2 MiB granularity (Ampere/Hopper, CUDA 12.x). Two taxes: 3 MiB + 1 B of request becomes 4 MiB of mappings (sub-page requests pay worst), and each call costs tens of µs to ms and can synchronize the device (order-of-magnitude, CUDA 12.x). A training step allocates thousands of tensors — nobody calls the driver per tensor, exactly as malloc shields the kernel (module 01 ch05).

First insider correction, derived: the driver can almost always find the bytes — physical pages may scatter, since each 2 MiB page maps independently. "Out of memory" usually means out of contiguous, policy-satisfiable memory: contiguous in one segment, right pool, right size class, splittable under the split rule. Those adjectives are the difference between a 3 a.m. crash and a boring run.

The caching allocator: ch19 replayed at device scale

PyTorch's CUDACachingAllocator (c10/cuda/CUDACachingAllocator.cpp, PyTorch 2.5) is ch19's segregated-fit, recognized, not reinvented:

  • The rounding ladder. Every request rounds to a 512 B multiple (kMinBlockSize). Requests ≤ 1 MiB go to the small pool, served from 2 MiB segments; requests > 1 MiB round to a 2 MiB multiple and go to the large pool, served from 20 MiB segments (kLargeBuffer). 512 B steps to 1 MiB, then 2 MiB steps forever: 1.5 MiB of data costs a 2 MiB block; 2.4 MiB costs 4 MiB.
  • Segregated free lists per size class, per pool: allocation is an exact-size hit or a best-fit-by-size search — no global walk. Ch19's whole point.
  • Split and remainder. A free block splits only if the remainder is itself legal (≥ 512 B small pool, ≥ 2 MiB large pool); a freed 5 MiB block serving a 4 MiB request is handed over whole — 1 MiB internal fragmentation, by policy.
  • Pools never mix. A 900 KiB request will not carve a large-pool free block; GiB of large-pool free space cannot save a small-pool allocation.

The critical vocabulary is three numbers and their guaranteed disagreement:

numbermeaningwho reports it
requestedsum of numel() * element_sizeyour mental math
allocatedsum of rounded block sizesmemory_summary
reservedsum of segment sizes held via cudaMallocnvidia-smi (+ context)

reserved ≥ allocated ≥ requested, always. nvidia-smi shows reserved plus a CUDA context (hundreds of MiB, CUDA 12.x) — why it disagrees with your tensor sum, and why 30 GiB of tensors OOMs a 40 GiB card. empty_cache() releases segments that are entirely free to the driver; it moves, merges, and defragments nothing inside partially-live segments — those are pinned by their live blocks. The lab shows it releasing 0 MiB at the moment of failure: the classic misuse.

The modern fix is expandable_segments (PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True, PyTorch 2.1+, opt-in as of 2.5): one large VA arena, with 2 MiB pages mapped/unmapped on demand (VMM APIs cuMemCreate/cuMemMap/cuMemUnmap, CUDA 10.2+). A freed block's pages unmap — physical memory returns to the driver immediately — and a later large request maps fresh pages into a fresh contiguous VA run. Contiguity becomes a choice made in VA space, not an accident of physical placement; the rounding tax remains.

Fragmentation arithmetic: free ≥ request, and it still fails

The chapter in one picture — the 40 MiB device the lab builds. Fill it with alternating tensors, four of 3 MiB + 1 B (4 MiB blocks) and four of 5 MiB + 1 B (6 MiB blocks), then free the 4 MiB side:

|   hole     tensor     hole     tensor     hole     tensor     |

               ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^  the span would have to cross a live tensor

Rule by rule: (1) exact size class — no 8 MiB free block exists, the lists hold 4 MiB blocks only; (2) best fit ≥ 8 MiB — largest hole is 4 MiB, fail; (3) coalesce — every hole's neighbors are live 6 MiB blocks, and adjacent free blocks would merge but no two free blocks are adjacent: module 01 ch05's lifetime interleaving, the no-move promise fencing the holes; (4) cross segments — never, they are separate VA reservations; (5) new segment — a fresh 20 MiB cudaMalloc against a device already 40/40 reserved: physical exhaustion, at the driver boundary. Free: 16 MiB. Request: 8 MiB. Failure — no byte was used up by the failing request; the bytes were stranded by geometry and policy. Scale it up (24 GiB card, 20–200 MiB holes, a 500 MiB request, thousands of segments) and you have the production OOM: "3.1 GiB is reserved by PyTorch but unallocated."

The real OOM: six causes

Fragmentation is cause #1 of six; the flowchart in How to use this knowledge walks the elimination:

  1. Fragmentation — reserved-but-unallocatable, as derived above; diagnosed by the "reserved but unallocated" line and a high reserved/allocated ratio.
  2. Other processes — the driver's pool is shared: nvidia-smi --query-compute-apps=pid,process_name,used_memory lists every co-tenant; a stale notebook or 3 a.m. cron job holds GiB. With MPS (multi-process service) clients share one server context — cheaper than a context each, but memory is attributed to the server process.
  3. NCCL communication buffers — every communicator in distributed training holds allreduce scratch: send and receive buffers per channel (2 × per channel), tens to hundreds of MiB (NCCL 2.x, scales with channels and GPU count), until process exit. Un-destroyed communicators are a slow leak with a networking-shaped cause.
  4. CUDA graph private pools — capture records the addresses of graph intermediates, so the allocator pins them in a pool owned by the graph; it stays reserved while the graph object lives, even if the tensors were "deleted."
  5. cuDNN workspaces — conv algorithm choice carries workspace memory: with torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark=True the fastest algorithm may want hundreds of MiB (FFT/Winograd variants, PyTorch 2.x) — transient, but it spikes reserved exactly when you were near the edge.
  6. Driver/kernel reserve — ECC, display, and driver structures eat memory before your first tensor: 0.5–1 GiB at boot on a 24 GiB GeForce card, a few percent more with ECC on datacenter cards (card- and CUDA-version dependent). Your real budget is total minus this reserve.

What insiders know

  • nvidia-smi's per-process number is reserved + context, not your tensors. The chain requested < allocated < reserved explains every "numbers disagree" mystery: first gap = the rounding tax summed, second = segment slack, then the context (hundreds of MiB, CUDA 12.x).
  • The size-class ladder, exactly (PyTorch 2.5): 512 B multiples to 1 MiB → small pool, 2 MiB segments; above 1 MiB, 2 MiB multiples → large pool, 20 MiB segments; large-pool split only when the remainder ≥ 2 MiB; pools never mix — a 900 KiB request starves next to a GiB of large-pool holes.
  • empty_cache() returns fully-free segments to the driver; it does not defragment and cannot help the OOM you are staring at — every segment holds a live block, which is what made it an OOM. Its real job: handing memory to co-tenants, at the cost of a device sync.
  • The OOM message is the diagnosis (PyTorch 2.x): "N GiB is reserved by PyTorch but unallocated" = fragmentation (cause 1); "Process P has X GiB memory in use" = co-tenants (cause 2); neither line and near-zero free = card genuinely full — causes 3–6 by elimination.
  • expandable_segments:True is the highest-leverage flag for fragmentation OOMs (PyTorch 2.1+): map/unmap 2 MiB pages in one VA arena instead of accumulating fixed cudaMalloc segments. Verify CUDA-graph-capture and NCCL interop notes for your exact release.
  • The six-cause triage order — fragmentation → other processes → NCCL → graph pools → cuDNN workspaces → driver reserve. Insiders spend the first two minutes on the error message and the process list, not on reinstalling.

See it yourself

All you need is Python (CPython 3.11 here; no GPU) — the allocator's policy, faithfully compressed, so you can watch the failure and its arithmetic:

# toy_alloc.py -- PyTorch's CUDACachingAllocator replayed on CPU (policy from
# c10/cuda/CUDACachingAllocator.cpp, PyTorch 2.5): small pool <= 1 MiB, 512 B
# rounding, 2 MiB segments; large pool, 2 MiB rounding, 20 MiB segments;
# size-class free lists; split only if the remainder is legal; adjacent frees
# coalesce; empty_cache() drops fully-free segments.
MB = 1 << 20

class OOM(Exception): pass

class Block:
    def __init__(s, seg, off, size):
        s.seg, s.off, s.size, s.free, s.req = seg, off, size, True, 0
    def __str__(s):
        return (f"seg{s.seg}[{s.off//MB}..{(s.off+s.size)//MB})M "
                f"{s.size//MB}M {'FREE' if s.free else 'LIVE'}")

def round_size(n):
    if n <= MB:  return (n + 511) // 512 * 512       # 512 B ladder
    return (n + 2*MB - 1) // (2*MB) * (2*MB)         # 2 MiB ladder

class Sim:
    def __init__(s, device):
        s.device, s.reserved = device, 0
        s.requested = s.allocated = 0                # the disagreeing sums
        s.segments, s.free_lists = [], {}            # 1 segment = 1 cudaMalloc

    def _cudaMalloc(s, size):                        # the driver boundary
        if s.reserved + size > s.device:
            free = sum(b.size for l in s.free_lists.values() for b in l)
            big = max((b.size for l in s.free_lists.values() for b in l), default=0)
            raise OOM(f"CUDA out of memory. Tried to allocate {size//MB} MiB "
                      f"(device {s.device//MB} MiB; {s.reserved//MB} MiB reserved; "
                      f"{free//MB} MiB is reserved but unallocated; "
                      f"largest free block {big//MB} MiB)")
        s.reserved += size
        s.segments.append([Block(len(s.segments), 0, size)])
        s._add(s.segments[-1][0])

    def _add(s, b):  s.free_lists.setdefault(b.size, []).append(b)
    def _rem(s, b):
        s.free_lists[b.size].remove(b)
        if not s.free_lists[b.size]: del s.free_lists[b.size]

    def _find(s, want):
        small = want <= MB                           # pools never mix
        if want in s.free_lists:
            for b in s.free_lists[want]:
                if (b.size <= MB) == small: return b
        c = [b for l in s.free_lists.values() for b in l
             if b.size >= want and (b.size <= MB) == small]
        return min(c, key=lambda b: b.size) if c else None

    def _split(s, b, want):
        rem = b.size - want
        if rem >= (512 if want <= MB else 2*MB):     # remainder must be legal
            tail = Block(b.seg, b.off + want, rem)
            s.segments[b.seg].append(tail); s._add(tail)
            b.size = want                            # split; remainder recycled
        # else: whole block handed over -> internal fragmentation, no split

    def alloc(s, n):
        want = round_size(n)
        b = s._find(want)
        if b is None:
            s._cudaMalloc(2*MB if want <= MB else max(20*MB, want))  # OOM born here
            b = s._find(want)
        s._rem(b); s._split(b, want); b.free = False; b.req = n
        s.requested += n; s.allocated += b.size
        return b

    def free(s, b):
        b.free = True
        s.allocated -= b.size; s.requested -= b.req
        seg = s.segments[b.seg]
        while True:                                  # coalesce adjacent frees
            nbr = next((o for o in seg if o is not b and o.free and
                        (o.off + o.size == b.off or b.off + b.size == o.off)), None)
            if nbr is None: break
            s._rem(nbr)
            lo, hi = (nbr, b) if nbr.off < b.off else (b, nbr)
            lo.size += hi.size; seg.remove(hi); b = lo
        s._add(b)

    def empty_cache(s):                              # fully-free segments only
        rel = 0
        for seg, blocks in enumerate(s.segments):
            if blocks and all(x.free for x in blocks):
                rel += blocks[0].size
                for b in blocks: s._rem(b)
                s.segments[seg] = []
        s.reserved -= rel
        return rel

What to notice: (1) the rounding tax — 32 MiB requested became 40 MiB allocated; (2) the OOM is raised at the driver boundary for a fresh 20 MiB segment while the message honestly reports 16 MiB reserved-but-unallocated, largest hole 4 MiB — free ≥ request, fail anyway; (3) a 4 MiB request succeeds (exact size class, hole reuse); (4) empty_cache() releases 0 MiB — both segments hold live 6 MiB blocks, so the "defragment with empty_cache" folklore dies right here. (Free everything and call it again: 40 MiB returns, and an 8 MiB allocation succeeds.) Now the same workload under the essence of expandable_segments — pages mapped/unmapped in one VA arena, physical returned at free time:

class SimExp:                                  # expandable_segments:True
    def __init__(s, device): s.device, s.mapped, s.va = device, 0, 0
    def alloc(s, n):
        want = round_size(n)                   # 2 MiB granularity tax remains
        if s.mapped + want > s.device: raise OOM("physical exhausted")
        s.mapped += want                       # driver maps fresh 2 MiB pages
        s.va += want                           # VA bump: endless, contiguous
        return s.va - want
    def free(s, n): s.mapped -= round_size(n)  # pages unmapped -> driver NOW

exp, As, Bs = SimExp(device=40*MB), [], []     # same workload
for _ in range(4):
    As.append(exp.alloc(3*MB + 1)); Bs.append(exp.alloc(5*MB + 1))
print("expandable fill:            mapped=%dM" % (exp.mapped//MB))
for a in As: exp.free(3*MB + 1)
print("expandable freed 4 MiB side: mapped=%dM (pages returned NOW)" % (exp.mapped//MB))
p = exp.alloc(8*MB)
print("expandable 8 MiB alloc:     OK at VA +%dM, mapped=%dM" % (p//MB, exp.mapped//MB))
expandable fill:            mapped=40M
expandable freed 4 MiB side: mapped=24M (pages returned NOW)
expandable 8 MiB alloc:     OK at VA +40M, mapped=32M

The 8 MiB allocation that OOM'd before now succeeds: freed pages left at free() time, and the request claimed a fresh contiguous VA run instead of hunting for a contiguous hole. That is the entire trick.

Read it in that order: requested → allocated → reserved, then the ratios and the OOM count. Inactive splits is the fragmentation census — blocks that freed next to live blocks, waiting for neighbors. A reserved/allocated ratio of 1.05 is normal churn; 1.3-plus with OOM events is cause #1 at production scale.

How to use this knowledge

The 3 a.m. flowchart — "CUDA out of memory at night," worked top to bottom:

 |

  • Read the error before touching anything. Its two lines (reserved-but-unallocated; other-process usage) do the first two flowchart steps for you — two minutes of reading beats an hour of superstitious restarts.
  • Capacity-plan by peak reserved, not tensor sum — peak reserved ≈ requested + rounding tax + segment slack + inactive splits + context. Two models with identical tensor sums can differ by GiB in reserved; only one fits.
  • Order fixes by cost: steady shapes (free) → expandable_segments:True (one env var) → smaller batch (throughput) → restart (last). empty_cache() is for handing memory to co-tenants, not for defragmenting yourself.
  • Interview whiteboard: "free ≥ request but the allocation fails — derive it." Draw the alternating-block picture, name the three rules (no coalescing across live blocks, no crossing segments, no split below the remainder floor), and you have demonstrated module 01 ch05, ch19, and this chapter in 90 seconds.

  6. The ratchet is module 01 ch05's external fragmentation: each step's different tensor sizes round to different block sizes; frees leave holes between live blocks; over thousands of steps the holes are too small or wrongly sized for current traffic, so reserved creeps up though every free is correct. empty_cache() cannot help: segments containing any live block are never released, and mid-loop nearly every segment does. Fixes: pad/cap variable batches so block sizes repeat and holes get exact-fit reuse; or PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True so freed pages return to the driver immediately and contiguity is chosen in VA. (Smaller batches also work, by fitting under the ceiling.)
  7. Compaction means moving blocks and updating every pointer to them — which requires enumerating all pointers. CUDA programs hold raw device pointers in tensors, graph-captured addresses (graphs pin exact addresses by design), and user-stashed data_ptr() values; the allocator can find and safely rewrite none of them. Module 01 ch05's deepest lesson, verbatim: fragmentation is the price of raw pointers — Java compacts because its runtime knows where every reference lives; the CUDA allocator cannot, because nobody does.
  8. It sits in the graph's private memory pool (cause #4): capture must keep every intermediate at a stable address for replay, so the allocator pins those blocks in a pool owned by the graph object, not the tensors. Deleting the tensors releases only user-facing handles; the pool stays reserved while the graph lives, and empty_cache() cannot release partially-pinned segments. The 1.4 GiB returns only when the graph object is destroyed and its pool drains.
