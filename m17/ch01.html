module 17 · the root under the root

From transistors to gates

chapter 01 · ~19 min read · deep-roots learning program
The 30-second version. A MOSFET is a voltage-controlled switch: the gate is a capacitor over the channel, and voltage above a threshold creates a conductive path from source to drain. NMOS conducts when its gate is high, PMOS when low — the complementarity of the dopings. One of each in series between VDD and GND, gates tied: the CMOS inverter, in which either steady state leaves one switch open — no DC path, static power is leakage only, a guarantee that died at 90 nm. Stack the switches as networks — 2 PMOS in parallel over 2 NMOS in series — and you get the NAND, 4 transistors, the cheapest 2-input gate, because CMOS is naturally inverting. Delay is RC: the 50% crossing sits at 0.69·RC, FO4 (~10-20 ps at 5-7 nm-class nodes) is the industry's unit of work, and a 3 GHz cycle is 15-20 FO4. Power is one equation, P = α·C·V²·f + V·I_leak: the V² term built DVFS, leakage ended the free gigahertz, both forced the multicore pivot of 2004. The clock is chapter 02's "data structure for time", bought with skew and a metastability MTBF cliff.

A voltage opens a channel: the MOSFET, and exactly where we stop

Module 01 chapter 01 gave you doped silicon and DRAM's capacitor-stored bits. One floor down: what is a transistor, at the minimum depth that still explains logic? The answer this module keeps: a voltage-controlled switch. The gate terminal is a metal plate on a thin oxide — a capacitor, the channel beneath as its other plate. Voltage on the gate pushes a field through the oxide; past a threshold Vt (roughly 0.2-0.4 V on modern logic processes, node-dependent) a conductive channel bridges source and drain. Below Vt the bridge is gone and only a nA-scale leak seeps. Above Vt the switch closes; below, it opens.

            gate terminal
                 |
           =============     gate plate  <- the capacitor's "top plate"
           -------------     thin oxide  <- the dielectric (nm-scale)
        +---------------------+
        |                     |
      source                drain
       (n+)                  (n+)        <- doped wells in a p-type body
        \____   channel   ____/
              (exists only when
               V_gate > Vt)

The two dopings give the two species: n-type wells in a p-type body (NMOS) form a channel when the gate is high — on at 1; p-type wells in an n-type body (PMOS) form one when the gate is low — on at 0. One is the photographic negative of the other, and that complementarity is the most load-bearing fact in this chapter.

Honesty checkpoint: below this — depletion regions, velocity saturation, why FinFETs exist — this module deliberately does not go. Chapters 02-05 derive correctly at the switch abstraction; deeper physics explains why Vt is what it is and changes none of the arithmetic. Process-dependent numbers get marked (5 nm-era-style), not derived.

The complementary pair: CMOS buys zero standing current

One PMOS from VDD, one NMOS to GND, gates tied, output at the middle: the inverter, the smallest complete thought in logic.

   in = 0                               in = 1
  ----------                            ----------
     VDD                                   VDD
      |                                     |
   [ PMOS ] closed                       [ PMOS ] open
      |                                     |
  out o------> out = 1                out o------> out = 0
      |                                     |
   [ NMOS ] open                        [ NMOS ] closed
      |                                     |
     GND                                   GND

Input high: NMOS conducts, PMOS off, output pulled to 0. Input low: PMOS conducts, NMOS off, output pulled to 1. The key property: in either steady state exactly one transistor is on — no DC path from VDD to GND. Current flows only while the output capacitance swings; static power is the off-transistor's leakage alone.

That leakage is the number that came back to collect. An off gate leaks nA-scale current (1-20 nA at 90 nm-class processes; Vt- and temperature-dependent): 10 nA × 10⁹ gates × 1.2 V ≈ 12 W of doing nothing, and 20 nA buys tens of watts. Before ~2000 leakage was under 1% of chip power; by 90 nm (2004-2006) it was 25-40% on hot designs (design-dependent, worse hot). The answers, readable in any datasheet: clock gating — stop toggling the clock to idle blocks, zeroing the dynamic term α·C·V²·f — and power gating — cut VDD with a sleep transistor, killing V·I_leak. The endpoint is dark silicon: Esmaeilzadeh et al. (ISCA 2011) projected 8 nm-era designs could afford to power under half the area they can print: transistors you cannot afford to turn on.

Switch networks and the price list: why NAND costs 4 and AND costs 6

A CMOS gate is two switch networks: a pull-up (PMOS) to VDD, a pull-down (NMOS) to GND. Complementarity makes the networks duals — PMOS in parallel wherever NMOS are in series — guaranteeing exactly one network closed for every input combination. The function falls out of the wiring. The NAND:

                VDD
                 |
        +--------+--------+
        |                 |
    a --o--[P1]       b --o--[P2]     PMOS pair in PARALLEL:
        |                 |               any 0 input closes one,
        +--------+--------+               pulls OUT high
                 |
               OUT ----------------->   out = 0  only when a=1 AND b=1
                 |
             [ N2 ] <-- gate = b
                 |
             [ N1 ] <-- gate = a        NMOS pair in SERIES:
                 |                       both 1 to pull OUT low
                GND

Read it as switches: a=0, b=1 closes P1 — output pulled to VDD, the NMOS chain broken at N1 regardless; a=1, b=1 opens both PMOS, closes both NMOS — output pulled to GND. NOT(a AND b), in 4 transistors. The NOR mirrors it (2 PMOS in series, 2 NMOS in parallel; also 4). The price list:

gatetransistorswhy
NOT2one pair
NAND2, NOR24the natural switch networks
AND2, OR26NAND/NOR + inverter
XOR212 static, 6 with transmission gatesthe parity tax

CMOS is naturally inverting — NAND, NOR, NOT are the cheap shapes; AND/OR pay 2 transistors and one extra 0.69·RC for the trailing inverter. Now kill the folklore: "NAND flash is dense because NAND gates are cheap." NAND flash is named for its array topology — cells strung in series, the same series-switch shape as the NAND's NMOS stack — and is dense because the string shares contacts (versus one per cell in the parallel NOR array). Two facts share a name for the same geometric reason; "NAND is denser" about gates conflates a flash story with a transistor count.

One more shape: an NMOS alone passes a strong 0 but a weak 1 (topping out near VDD − Vt); a PMOS passes a strong 1 and a weak 0. In parallel, driven by complementary signals, each covers the other's weak level: the transmission gate, a switch passing both logic levels — the trick behind pass-transistor logic's cheaper muxes and XOR (a 2:1 mux: 6 with transmission gates, ~12 static), and behind chapter 04's SRAM write path.

Delay is RC: 0.69, FO4, and the end of free gigahertz

Gate delay is not the transistor "being slow" — it is the transistor charging a capacitor through a resistance. The output drives every fanout gate's input pin (~0.1-0.3 fF each at 7 nm-class nodes) plus the wire — ~0.15-0.25 fF/µm on intermediate metal, so a 200 µm route adds ~40 fF, more than the pins it connects. The conducting transistor is a kΩ-scale resistor. RC into a capacitor gives the exponential curve:

 VDD |                        .-----------------   V(t) = VDD(1 - e^(-t/RC))
     |                  , ' - '
     |              , '
 VDD/2 - - - - x- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -     x: V crosses 50%
     |        ,'    :                             at t = RC·ln(2) = 0.69RC
     |     ,'      :
   0 +--'---------:--------------------------> t
     0            tau = RC   (63% at one tau)

Derive the 50% crossing once and own it: VDD(1 − e^(−t/RC)) = VDD/2, so e^(−t/RC) = 1/2, so t = RC·ln 2 = 0.693·RC. Every timing analyzer and PVT corner below is this equation dressed up.

Drive strength is where design lives. A transistor 2× wider has ~½ the on-resistance — it drives the same load ~2× faster — but its own gate pin is ~2× the capacitance, so whoever drives it pays 2×. The logical-effort calculus (Sutherland, Sproull, Harris, 1999) puts the optimum at each stage driving 2.7-4× its own input capacitance: 2.7 = e^1 is the zero-parasitic result, real parasitics push toward 4. That fixes the industry's unit of work: the FO4 delay — one inverter driving 4 identical ones — ~10-20 ps at 5-7 nm-class nodes (foundry-dependent). Insiders say "this adder is 15 FO4", not "3 ns": FO4 ports across nodes because it is normalized to the silicon's own speed.

Cycle-time arithmetic explains a decade of history. A 3 GHz clock is a 333 ps period: at ~20 ps per FO4, ~17 FO4 per cycle — budgets are quoted as 15-20 FO4, covering logic, wires, skew, and margin. More GHz means fewer FO4 per stage, less logic per stage, a deeper pipeline: the Pentium 4 — Willamette's 20 stages, Prescott's 31, clocks to 3.8 GHz — until the power equation below killed the successor (Tejas, cancelled 2004), while a branch mispredict flushed ~20-39 mostly-empty stages. Chapter 02 formalizes that trade; here the lesson is that GHz stopped climbing because 15-20 FO4 is what a coolable cycle buys, not because transistors stopped switching.

The two-term bill: αCV²f + V·I_leak, and why multicore happened

Derive the dynamic term like the 0.69. Charge output capacitance C from 0 to VDD through the pull-up resistance: the supply moves Q = C·VDD, so it delivers energy C·VDD²; the capacitor stores ½·C·VDD²; the other ½·C·VDD² burns in the resistance — independent of the resistance value (the physics of the loss). Discharge dumps the stored half as heat too. So every 0→1 transition costs the supply CV². With the fraction of cycles in which a node makes a 0→1 transition — the activity factor α, 0.01-0.2 in real logic — the chip's bill is the equation that ran the 2000s:

P  =  α·C·V²·f  +  V·I_leak
     ^^^^^^^^^^     ^^^^^^^^
     dynamic:       static:
     transitions    the off-switch leak
     per second     (nA-scale × 10⁹, section 2)

The dynamic term's V² is the lever: 1.2 V → 0.9 V drops dynamic power to (0.9/1.2)² = 0.5625 of its old self — a 44% cut for the same clock and logic, from one exponent. That fact is the entire DVFS industry (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling): your laptop's governor drops V and f together, because lower V means slower gates — the triangle below — so frequency must follow voltage down.

The bill is also why your CPU has cores. The 2004 plan was a 4 GHz+ single core, and planar physics said no: the required voltage and leakage could not be cooled at sane cost — the power wall. The levers: V² says lower voltage (f follows); α says gate the idle; leakage says a multibillion-transistor single core pays nA-scale rent on everything. The industry took the intersection: two slower cores at lower V beat one fast core within the same watts, with software supplying the parallelism — module 09's reason for existing, and module 12's GPU bet (throughput per watt, thousands of simpler cores), played out.

Voltage, temperature, frequency are a triangle. Lower V → lower drive current → each 0.69·RC stretches → margin shrinks; heat makes both worse. Foundries force it with PVT corners: signoff at the worst combinations — "slow transistors, 0.72 V, 125 °C junction"-style, values node- and foundry-dependent — not the typical one. Ships have margin, and binning is that margin's business model: one design, corner-sorted, sold as 3.5/4.0/4.5 GHz SKUs. The enthusiast's undervolt is the consumer-side arbitrage of the same margin — harvesting slack the foundry made the vendor leave — and it works until a 0.72 V-class, 110 °C-class day finds you, which is why undervolting folklore and warranties disagree.

The clock: discrete time, skew, and the metastability cliff

Combinational logic just maps inputs to outputs; state needs time chopped into steps, and hardware's answer is the clock. The alternative exists — asynchronous, self-timed logic with completion handshakes (the AMULET asynchronous ARM research chips) — and can be energy-efficient. It lost anyway, for a precise reason: verifiability. One global tick turns a continuum of analog waveforms into discrete cycle numbers — every flip-flop samples at edge N, results are consumed at edge N+1, and "when did this wire settle?" becomes "by which edge?" The phrasing chapter 02 reuses: a clock is a data structure for time. It costs two diseases.

Disease one: skew. The tick must reach every flip-flop through repeater buffers — the clock tree, drawn as an H so every leaf sees equal wire length — and still arrives with 10s of ps of spread across a die, each ps stolen from chapter 02's cycle budget. The PLL multiplies a quartz reference up (crystals are stable but 10s of MHz; 100 MHz × 40 = 4 GHz). Multiple domains — CPU, memory controller, each with its own PLL — create the boundary where disease two lives.

        quartz 100 MHz --> [PLL x40] --> 4 GHz tick
                                |
                            [buffer]
                          /          \
                     [buffer]      [buffer]        the H-tree: equal
                    /       \      /       \       wire lengths, so the
                  ...       ...  ...       ...    tick arrives together
                (10^5 - 10^7 flip-flop clock pins; the tree burns
                 20-40% of dynamic power on large designs)

Disease two: metastability. A signal crossing clock domains is sampled at arbitrary phase; sample it in the window where it crosses 50% of VDD — the setup/hold window, chapter 02's topic — and the flip-flop's internal cross-coupled pair can balance at half-voltage for an indeterminate time before resolving. Nothing is broken: a bistable element was asked to arbitrate an analog race, and resolution time is exponential, not bounded. The fix is the two-flop synchronizer: the first flop may go metastable, but gets a full period to resolve before the second samples. The arithmetic (Wakerly's form) is the design rule:

MTBF = e^(tr/tau) / (f_clk · f_data)

tr  = resolution time offered (period - setup; ~1.8 ns on a 2 ns clock)
tau = the flop's metastability time constant (process-dependent:
      ~10-20 ps on modern nodes, ~50 ps on a 2000s-era node)

Run real numbers and watch the cliff. f_clk = 500 MHz, f_data = 10 MHz, tr = 1.8 ns. With τ = 50 ps: tr/τ = 36, e³⁶ ≈ 4×10¹⁵, MTBF ≈ 4×10¹⁵/(5×10⁸ · 10⁷) ≈ 0.86 s — fails about every second. With τ = 20 ps: tr/τ = 90, e⁹⁰ ≈ 1.2×10³⁹, MTBF ≈ 2.4×10²³ s ≈ 7.7×10¹⁵ years — 500,000× the age of the universe. Same circuit, τ halved, MTBF multiplied by e^54: that exponent is why the rule is two flops, never one, and why folklore says "add a flop if the MTBF is within a few orders of magnitude of comfortable."

Multi-bit buses break the two-flop fix: each bit resolves independently, so a sampled word can tear — half old, half new. The rule: cross one bit at a time (a handshake flag, itself two-flop synchronized), and move multi-bit counters in Gray code, where one bit changes per increment: 7→8 is 0b0111→0b1000 in binary (four bits) but 0b0100→0b1100 in Gray (one bit) — a torn sample is old or new, never a third value. That trick is the async FIFO pointer discipline chapter 03 implements in Verilog.

What insiders know

  • Transistor counts are the interview party trick: inverter 2, NAND2/NOR2 4, AND2/OR2 6, XOR2 12 static or 6 with transmission gates — and the reason (parallel vs series networks, naturally inverting) is what interviewers probe.
  • Budgets are quoted in FO4, never ns: "this pipeline stage is 15 FO4"; ~10-20 ps per FO4 at 5-7 nm-class nodes, 333 ps per 3 GHz cycle = 15-20 FO4, and any block above ~20 FO4 is shipping a clock compromise.
  • Leakage was a rounding error until 90 nm, then 25-40% of power (hot, design-dependent); clock gating kills α·CV²f, power gating kills V·I_leak, dark silicon (>50% unaffordable area at 8 nm-class, ISCA 2011) is the endpoint.
  • "NAND is denser" folklore conflates flash array topology (series string, shared contacts) with the logic price list (4 transistors). Two true facts, one shared name, zero causal link.
  • Signoff happens at PVT corners like slow/0.72 V/125 °C, not nominal; binning (3.5/4.0/4.5 GHz from one design) sells the margin back by speed grade, and undervolting harvests it personally — until the corner finds you.
  • Metastability MTBF moves by e^(tr/τ): 500 MHz/10 MHz/1.8 ns swings from 0.86 s (τ = 50 ps) to 7.7×10¹⁵ years (τ = 20 ps). Never cross a domain with one flop; never cross a bus without Gray code or a handshake.
  • kΩ·fF = ps exactly (10³ Ω × 10⁻¹⁵ F = 10⁻¹² s): the head-check for any RC delay, and the calibration of this chapter's simulator.

The simulator. An event-driven gate simulator: gates are functions with delays, time is a priority queue, delay is 0.69·R·C in code — and a pulse narrower than a gate's delay is swallowed (the inertial rule), the software image of "never crossed the 50% threshold". Units: kΩ·fF = ps; a unit pin is 1 fF, a unit pull 7.2 kΩ, so one FO4 = 0.69·7.2·4 ≈ 20 ps (5 nm-era-style; real 5-7 nm FO4 spans 10-20 ps).

# gatesim.py -- an event-driven gate simulator with RC delays.
# Units: resistance kOhm, capacitance fF, time ps. kOhm*fF = ps, exactly.
# A gate's delay = 0.69 * R * C_load. Pulses shorter than a gate's delay
# are swallowed (inertial delay): "never crossed the 50% threshold".
import heapq

class Sim:
    def __init__(self):
        self.q, self.seq, self.gen = [], 0, {}
        self.net, self.fan, self.log = {}, {}, []

    def gate(self, out, fn, r, *ins):
        """One gate: output net `out`, logic fn over inputs `ins`,
        pull resistance r in kOhm (smaller r = stronger gate)."""
        for n in ins:
            self.fan.setdefault(n, []).append((out, fn, r, ins))

    def cap(self, net):                       # load on a net: 1 fF per pin
        return float(len(self.fan.get(net, [])))

    def set(self, net, v, t=0.0):             # drive an input net at time t
        self.seq += 1
        heapq.heappush(self.q, (t, self.seq, net, v, None))

    def _fire(self, g, t):                    # g's inputs just changed at t
        out, fn, r, ins = g
        v = fn(*[self.net.get(i, 0) for i in ins])
        self.gen[out] = self.gen.get(out, 0) + 1     # cancel pending output
        if v != self.net.get(out):
            self.seq += 1
            heapq.heappush(self.q, (t + 0.69 * r * self.cap(out),
                                    self.seq, out, v, self.gen[out]))

    def run(self, until=1e9):
        while self.q and self.q[0][0] <= until:
            t, _, net, v, gen = heapq.heappop(self.q)
            if gen is not None and gen != self.gen.get(net):   # cancelled
                continue
            if v == self.net.get(net):
                continue
            self.net[net] = v
            self.log.append((round(t, 2), net, v))
            for g in self.fan.get(net, []):
                self._fire(g, t)

INV  = lambda a: 1 - a
NAND = lambda a, b: 0 if (a and b) else 1

Exercise 1 — the chain of 4, and where it stops. Each stage delays 0.69·7.2·1 = 4.97 ps. Sweep the input period:

from gatesim import Sim, INV

def chain4(period_ps, until=300.0):
    s = Sim()
    for out, i in [("w1","in"),("w2","w1"),("w3","w2"),("w4","w3")]:
        s.gate(out, INV, 7.2, i)
    t = 0.0
    while t <= until:                    # square wave of given period
        s.set("in", 1, t); s.set("in", 0, t + period_ps/2)
        t += period_ps
    s.run(until)
    return sum(1 for tt, n, v in s.log if n == "w4")

for T in (40, 20, 12, 10, 8):
    print(f"period {T:3d} ps (half {T//2:2d}): w4 edges {chain4(T):3d}")
period  40 ps (half 20): w4 edges  15
period  20 ps (half 10): w4 edges  29
period  12 ps (half  6): w4 edges  48
period  10 ps (half  5): w4 edges  58
period   8 ps (half  4): w4 edges   0

Propagation dies between half-period 5 and 4 ps — within 1 ps of the 4.97 ps stage delay: a pulse narrower than 0.69·RC never crosses the 50% threshold. The RC limit, observed in software; your CPU's maximum clock is this experiment scaled up.

Exercise 2 — measure an FO4 like silicon does (real chips use ring oscillators): 5 inverters in a ring, each node loaded with 4 unit pins.

s = Sim()
names = [f"w{i}" for i in range(5)]
for i in range(5):
    s.gate(names[i], INV, 7.2, names[i-1])       # the ring wiring
    for k in range(3):                           # 3 dummy pins: 4 per node
        s.gate(f"pad{i}_{k}", lambda a: 0, 7.2, names[i])
s.net["w1"] = 1; s.net["w3"] = 1                 # mid-flight snapshot
s.set("w0", 1, 0.0)                              # the kick that starts it
s.run(until=900.0)
ts = [t for t, n, v in s.log if n == "w0"]
period = ts[-1] - ts[-3]
print("period =", round(period,1), "ps; FO4 = period/(2*5) =",
      round(period/10, 2), "ps")
period = 198.7 ps; FO4 = period/(2*5) = 19.87 ps

The edge must lap the 5-stage ring twice to restore polarity, so period = 2·N·t_pd and FO4 = period/10 ≈ 19.9 ps — a 3 GHz cycle is 333/19.9 ≈ 17 of them.

Exercise 3 — the thermal wall on paper. One gate, C = 10 fF (a moderately loaded output), V = 1.0 V, f = 1 GHz, α = 0.1:

C, V, f, alpha = 10e-15, 1.0, 1e9, 0.1
P = alpha * C * V**2 * f
print(P*1e6, "uW/gate;", P*1e3*1e3, "mW per 1,000-gate block;",
      P*1e9, "W per billion gates")
1.0 uW/gate; 1.0 mW per 1,000-gate block; 1000.0 W per billion gates

1 µW per gate is nothing; a billion gates at those parameters is 1,000 W — the wall on paper, before leakage and the clock tree. Every "why is my CPU capped at 5 GHz" reduces to this arithmetic plus section 5's second term.

Exercise 4 — the metastability cliff. Evaluate math.exp(1.8e-9/tau)/(5e8*1e7) for τ = 50e-12 and 20e-12: 0.86 s and 7.7×10¹⁵ years — same circuit, two processes, a factor of e^54. Repeat with tr = 0.2 ns and watch the good τ collapse too.

On your own machine (Linux laptop): grep MHz /proc/cpuinfo idle and under load — the DVFS governor trading V² for speed; if RAPL is exposed, read /sys/class/powercap/intel-rapl:0/energy_uj twice one second apart and subtract — your CPU's watts, measured by the chip.

How to use this knowledge

  • Performance claims decode into FO4. "3 GHz" is 333 ps = 15-20 FO4 per cycle; "mispredict costs ~20 cycles" is 20 × 17 FO4 flushed. A GPU vendor quoting half a CPU's clocks is pricing thermal headroom.
  • The hierarchy gets units. A DRAM access is ~100 ns ≈ 5,000 FO4 ≈ 300 cycles — one division that explains why module 01 ch02's cache line dominates software performance.
  • Benchmark variance stops being mysterious. Thermal throttling at a ~100 °C-class junction, governor voltage steps, and corner-margin binning are section 5's equation running on your desk; two "identical" SKUs are two draws from one corner distribution.
  • Whiteboard screens. Derive 0.69·RC, derive αCV²f from the ½CV²-vs-CV² bookkeeping, name why NAND is 4 transistors and AND is 6 — each a 90-second derivation and a standard interview question.
  • Datacenter power debugging. A server drawing real watts at 0% load is not broken: leakage plus a clock tree burning 20-40% of dynamic (large-design range) are toggling whether or not anyone is home. The fix is architectural, so capacity planning owns it.

Answers
  1. Zero DC current — one transistor of each type is off. The pull-up and pull-down are complementary duals, so exactly one network conducts per steady input: no VDD-to-GND path, only the off-transistors' nA leakage. At 90 nm, 1-20 nA per gate × 10⁹ gates × ~1.2 V reaches tens of watts (25-40% of power on hot designs). The responses: clock gating (zero the α·C·V²·f term for idle blocks) and power gating (cut VDD to kill V·I_leak); dark silicon is the endpoint.
  1. The NAND is the natural switch network; the AND buys an inverter. NAND: 2 PMOS in parallel (any 0 input closes one, pulling the output high) over 2 NMOS in series (both 1 to pull low) — duality guarantees one closed network per input, 4 transistors. AND = NAND + inverter = 6 transistors and one extra 0.69·RC. The bias: CMOS is naturally inverting — NOT/NAND/NOR are minimal; AND/OR pay for the trailing inversion.
  1. The pull-up is closed through P1; the output is 1. a = 0 turns P1 on (PMOS conducts when its gate is low), connecting the output to VDD; the parallel PMOS pair makes P2 irrelevant, and the NMOS chain is broken at N1 (an NMOS needs its gate high). Output = 1 = NAND(0,1), with no DC current drawn.
  1. Factor 0.5625 — a 44% cut (43.75%). Dynamic power is α·C·V²·f, so at fixed f and workload the ratio is (0.9/1.2)² = 0.5625. The symbol is the squared V — the exponent behind the DVFS industry.
  1. A pulse must outlive one stage delay. The input half-period is the pulse width at stage 1. At 5 ps it just exceeds the 4.97 ps delay, so the pending output event fires before the return edge cancels it and walks the chain; at 4 ps the next input edge arrives first, the simulator cancels the pending transition (the gen bump), and the pulse dies. The inertial rule models RC reality: VDD(1 − e^(−t/RC)) never reaches the 50% threshold if the input reverses first — scaled up, a cycle shorter than the path's RC is a clock the logic cannot answer.
  1. A PVT corner escape. Low supply cuts drive current, stretching every 0.69·RC; heat slows transistors further and inflates leakage. Nominal-condition tests (1.0 V-class, 25 °C) validate a corner the field never visits; signoff requires closure at the worst corner — slow transistors, ~0.72 V, 125 °C-style (values vary by node and foundry). The business practice is binning: one design, corner-sorted, sold as 3.5/4.0/4.5 GHz SKUs, so the spread becomes a lineup instead of a recall. Undervolting is the private version of the same arbitrage; the hot-room failure is its failure mode.
  1. The bus tore. Two-flop synchronizers fix per-bit metastability, but pointer bits resolve independently. On 7→8, binary 0b0111→0b1000 changes four bits at once; mid-flight sampling can deliver 0b1111 or 0b0000 — values the counter never held — so the FIFO computes a bogus full/empty decision and over/underflows later (rarely enough to pass testing, MTBF-style). The fix: cross one bit at a time via a handshake flag (itself two-flop synchronized), and carry the pointer in Gray code — 7→8 is a single-bit change, 0b0100→0b1100 — so a torn sample is old or new, never a third value.
  1. ~5,000 FO4. 100 ns / 20 ps = 5,000; at ~17 FO4 per 3 GHz cycle that is ~300 cycles, matching module 01 ch02. A single DRAM access costs thousands of FO4 against a 15-20 FO4 per-cycle budget, so the hierarchy exists to keep most accesses on chip — module 01's cycle counts, now priced in the physics that produces them.
