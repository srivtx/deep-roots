Module 14 final exam

every chapter · worked answers behind the fold

Twelve questions across the module's five chapters. Closed book; every answer is worked, not stated — the derivations are the point. Scoring: 11+ = you own this module; 8-10 = solid, revisit the flagged chapters; <8 = the chapter quizzes are the study path.

Part I — the medium's arithmetic (chapter 01)

  1. Predict. A service must sustain 50,000 random 4-KiB read IOPS. Derive the fleet size for 7,200 rpm SATA spindles (12 ms average seek, 200 MB/s outer track) and for 15,000 rpm SAS spindles (3.5 ms seek), then give the single-device answer — and the precondition the application must satisfy before that one device delivers anything near its number.
  1. Diagnose. Two identical 2 TB TLC drives run the same nightly 300 GB backup. The drive at 30% full starts at 2.5 GB/s and settles at 900 MB/s; the drive at 93% full bursts only briefly, then collapses to 150 MB/s — slower than a 7,200 rpm HDD reads. Nothing is failing on either drive. Name both mechanisms with their numbers, and the one fix that addresses them together.
  1. Derive. (a) Derive, from NAND physics, why an FTL can never overwrite a logical page in place — and name the object that constraint forces into existence, with its DRAM price for a 1 TB drive of 4 KiB pages. (b) A 100%-full TLC drive, 16 KiB pages, 128 pages per erase block, every page valid, TRIM never issued, no spare blocks: the host overwrites one logical page. Count the flash page-writes, the WAF, and the NAND bytes. (c) The drive is rated 600 TBW at WAF 2.5. A cache workload pins WAF at 8 — what is the real host-write budget?

Part II — objects, cache, and the fsync contract (chapter 02)

  1. Diagnose. A 100 GB volume: df -h / says 100% used, but du -sx / accounts for only 61 GB. A log daemon has been up for weeks, and rotation is a nightly rm of the old log. Where are the other ~39 GB, which two counts must both reach zero before they free, why can the held space still grow — and why is du structurally blind to any of it?
  1. Predict. A single-threaded importer writes 40 GB to a fresh file on a 32 GB RAM server with every default (vm.dirty_ratio 20, vm.dirty_background_ratio 10, writeback cadence 5 s, page age 30 s). Predict the run: how the first ~3 GB feel, at what Dirty level the writer stalls and inside which kernel function, and the standard fix with its trade.
  1. Diagnose. A config saver does: write a temp file, fsync(tempfd), rename(temp, config). After a power cut the OLD config is back. The developer protests: "I fsynced before the rename!" What exactly did that fsync make durable, what did it not touch, why is close() never a substitute — and what is the complete recipe?

Part III — the journaling lineage (chapter 03)

  1. Predict. A 3 GiB file is written in one streaming pass, perfectly contiguous, 4 KiB blocks — once on ext4, once on ext3. For each: how many records or pointer blocks map the file, does the map fit inside the inode, and what does reading the file's last KiB cost in extra block reads before the data arrives?
  1. Diagnose. A mail server runs ext4 mounted data=writeback "for speed." After a crash, recovered mailboxes contain long stretches of a build tarball deleted the previous day. Derive the exact sequence writeback permits, why data=ordered — the default — structurally cannot produce it, why writeback is faster anyway, and where data=journal sits on the same axis.

Part IV — the copy-on-write lineage (chapter 04)

  1. Predict. One small random overwrite of an existing file: (a) 4 KiB on Btrfs at tree depth 2, then at depth 3 (16 KiB nodes, plus the rotated superblock); (b) 8 KiB on OpenZFS defaults — recordsize 128 KiB, ARC miss, fsync; (c) 4 KiB on ext4 ordered mode. Derive each byte count and amplification factor — then name the two reasons this write shape nevertheless suits the flash underneath.
  1. Predict. A 2 TiB Btrfs subvolume carries one read-only snapshot. Since the snapshot, the live subvolume has overwritten 5 GB of existing files — nothing appended, nothing deleted. (a) You delete the snapshot: how much space frees, and why? (b) A second team, identical setup except their live tree only appended 10 GB after the snapshot, deletes theirs and nothing frees. Derive both outcomes from the refcount rule.

Part V — special file systems and the one-write picture (chapter 05)

  1. Diagnose. A Docker image bakes a 100 MB model file into a layer; a container edits 1 byte of it at startup, and the writable layer — and boot time — balloons. Predict the write volume and walk the mechanism: where the bytes come from, why the edit cannot land in place, what deleting that file would do instead, and the fix. Name the module 01 ch03 primitive this replays, and its chapter 04 cousin.
  1. Concept (cross-chapter). "The file system writes your bytes to the disk." Attack the claim with the module's one-write diagram: walk write(fd, buf, 4096) on an ext4-over-NVMe file box by box, tagging each with its module 14 chapter; state exactly where write() returns and the four events that can push the page down later; then walk the reclaim loop that fires when memory pressure arrives before writeback does — including which pages take the swap branch.
Answers

Part I

  1. 7,200 rpm: rotation 8.33 ms, average wait 4.17; seek 12; transfer 4 KiB / 200 MB/s = 0.02 → 16.19 ms → 1000/16.19 = 62 IOPS per spindle → 50,000/62 ≈ 807 spindles. 15,000 rpm: rotation 4 ms, average wait 2; 3.5 + 2 + 0.02 = 5.52 ms → 181 IOPS → 277 spindles. Or one NVMe drive at QD32 (150,000-400,000 IOPS) — if the queue is actually deep: a synchronous issue-read-wait-respond program is QD1 by construction and gets 1/75 µs ≈ 13,300 IOPS — a quarter of the target from a million-IOPS drive. Depth requires many threads or io_uring (Linux 5.1+); a 32-deep queue on the HDD only buys the elevator's 100-200 IOPS — reordering cannot fix mechanics (ch01).
  1. Mechanism one, the pseudo-SLC cache: consumer TLC reserves 12-100 GB programmed as fast single-bit cells, and it is carved from free NAND — the 93%-full drive has lost roughly an order of magnitude of it, so burst and sustained rates collapse together. Mechanism two, GC write amplification: past ~90% occupancy the blocks the garbage collector recycles are mostly valid, so each host write drags 5-10 flash writes behind it (1.5-3 on a healthy drive) and internal bandwidth saturates before the host's — 900 MB/s ÷ ~6 ≈ the 150 observed. One fix for both: leave 10-20% of the drive unpartitioned (overprovisioning) or buy enterprise spare — mostly garbage blocks let the GC erase cheaply and leave the cache its room (ch01).
  1. (a) Programming drives bits only 1-to-0 at page granularity; restoring a 1 requires an erase, and erase granularity is the whole block — an in-place overwrite would destroy the 127 neighboring valid pages. So the new version lands in a fresh page and something must remember which physical page answers for a logical address: the LPA-to-PPA map, ~4 B per page — 1 TB / 4 KiB = 268M entries ≈ 1 GiB of DRAM, why 1 TB drives carry 1 GB and DRAM-less ones stutter on random access. (b) Copy the 127 still-valid pages, write the new version, erase: 128 flash page-writes for one host page — WAF 128, and 128 × 16 KiB = 2 MiB of NAND for a 16 KiB write (the chapter's 64x corner is the 64-page-block case; worst-case WAF equals pages per block). (c) TBW = capacity × P/E / WAF: 1 TB × 1,500 / 8 = 187.5 TB of host writes, not 600 — each unit of amplification drains the P/E budget on a schedule the host-write calendar cannot see (Percentage Used can: it folds in the drive's measured WAF) (ch01).

Part II

  1. The ~39 GB live in deleted-but-open files. rm is unlink(): the dentry is removed and i_nlink drops to 0 — but the daemon's fd still references the inode (i_count ≥ 1), and blocks are freed only when i_nlink = 0 AND the last fd closes. The daemon keeps appending into the now-nameless inode, so the held 39 GB can still grow. du walks names, and these blocks' only pointer is a process's fd table. lsof +L1 lists exactly the link-count-0 files still held open; the fix is restarting (or signaling) the daemon, or rotating by truncation instead of unlink (ch02).
  1. The first ~3.2 GB (the 10% background threshold) complete at memcpy speed — write() returns from the dirty page, zero I/O. Background flushers wake at 3.2 GB and drain concurrently; when Dirty crosses 20% of 32 GB = 6.4 GB, the writer itself is drafted into synchronous writeback inside balance_dirty_pages() — D state, blocked in write() in device-rate-sized bursts until the level falls, repeatedly across a 40 GB run (pages may legally sit dirty 30 s; flusher cadence 5 s). Fix: vm.dirty_bytes, with dirty_background_bytes lower (tens to hundreds of MB) — writeback starts early and throttling stays brief. The trade: small frequent stalls bought against rare multi-second freezes — cadence for tail latency (ch02).
  1. The fsync made the file durable: its data blocks plus the metadata needed to find them (size and extent updates — delayed allocation resolved right now) plus the device-cache flush. It touched nothing about the rename — that is directory data, journaled metadata that commits within ext4's 5 s interval. Power died inside the window: replay restored the old directory entry, so the old name points at the old content and the fsynced new blocks are an orphan no name reaches. close() flushes nothing by POSIX design — it is never a durability tool. The recipe: write temp → fsync(tempfd) → rename → fsync(dirfd of the final path) — the last step makes the rename itself durable, and it is the one most production code omits. (auto_da_alloc covers the never-fsynced zero-length case, not this one) (ch02; ch03's commit interval).

Part III

  1. ext4: 3 GiB / 128 MiB per extent = 24 extents — 288 B of records. The inode's 60-byte i_block holds a 12-B header plus 4 × 12-B entries — 4 extents maximum — so the tree grows one level: a single 4 KiB leaf block (each holds ~340 extents) carries all 24. Map cost: 1 metadata block; the tail read walks inode → leaf → data = 1 extra read. ext3: 786,432 blocks — 12 direct + 1,024 single-indirect, the remaining 785,396 under double indirection: 767 second-level pointer blocks plus the top one = 768 pointer blocks ≈ 3 MiB, 768x the ext4 map, and the same tail read pays top plus one second-level = 2 extra reads ≈ 24 ms of pointer chasing at 12 ms per HDD access — every tail access, forever (ch03).
  1. Writeback journals metadata only, with no data ordering: the mailbox grew, the allocator handed it the tarball's freshly freed blocks, the extent update was journaled and committed before those blocks were ever written; power died; replay faithfully restored pointers to blocks still holding the tarball's old bytes. Ordered mode structurally cannot produce this: at commit, JBD2 forces every dirty data block of the transaction's files to its final location before the metadata enters the journal — committed pointers never lead unwritten data; you may lose a file's tail, you can never gain another file's bytes. Writeback is faster precisely because it skips that forced flush. data=journal is the far end of the axis — data rides the log too, a crash can cost only the final seconds of log, but every byte is written twice (~2x I/O), worth it only for special volumes (ch03).

Part IV

  1. (a) Btrfs, depth 2: 4 KiB data + 16 KiB leaf + 16 KiB root + 4 KiB superblock = 40 KiB = 10x; depth 3 adds one 16 KiB internal node: 56 KiB = 14x (both before compression and csum-tree updates). (b) ZFS: CoW rewrites whole records — the 8 KiB page forces read 128 KiB + write 128 KiB (plus the metadata path and a small ZIL record so fsync can return) = 16x write amplification and 16x the read traffic. (c) ext4 ordered: the 4 KiB data block plus 2-3 journal blocks — O(1) metadata per update. That arithmetic is the measured 2-5x gap for fixed random overwrites at scale. The coda: CoW likes flash — fresh-block writes never trigger the FTL's read-modify-erase cycle, and freed extents pair with TRIM; the insult is two log-structured allocators now hiding each other's assumption of contiguity (ch04; ch01).
  1. (a) ~5 GB frees. The overwrites allocated fresh blocks for the live tree; the old 5 GB of blocks stayed valid because the snapshot's tree still points at them — reference count 1, the snapshot their only holder. Deleting the snapshot drops those counts to 0: freed (the snapshot's own footprint is a few KiB of metadata). (b) ~0 frees. The appended 10 GB belongs to the live tree alone — the snapshot predates it and never referenced it — and every block the snapshot does hold is still shared with the live tree (count 2 → 1). The rule: a snapshot is a saved root pointer plus a refcount on every extent it reaches; creating one costs 0 data bytes, and deleting one gives back only what churn — overwrites and deletes after the snapshot — made exclusively its own (ch04).

Part V

  1. ~100 MB, slightly over — data plus mode, ownership, and xattrs/ACLs. The file lives in a read-only lower layer shared with every container from that image, so the edit cannot land in place: overlayfs copy-up hoists the entire file into the container's upperdir, and only then applies the byte — a 1-byte edit billed at whole-file granularity, once per container per file. Deleting the file could not touch the lower layer either: the upperdir gets a whiteout — a character-device entry, major 0, minor 0 — that hides the name (one rm of a file present in N layers performs N layer operations). Fix: a volume or bind mount for the big mutable file, bypassing the layer stack. The primitive is module 01 ch03's MAP_PRIVATE never-modify-the-shared-original; chapter 04's reflink is the same rule at extent granularity (ch05; ch04).
  1. fd 3 indexes the fd table to a struct file (position, flags, refcount) — ch02; the path resolves through the dentry cache (the name) — ch02; the dentry points at the inode (identity, extent-tree root — on-disk layout from ch03); the 4 KiB page at (inode, offset) is marked dirty and write() returns here — zero I/O has happened. The four pushes: fsync; Dirty crossing vm.dirty_ratio (20% of RAM); the 30 s age against the 5 s writeback cadence; memory-pressure reclaim. Then ext4 resolves delayed allocation and JBD2 logs the metadata first — ch03; a bio (device, LBA, page) enters the block layer (none scheduler on NVMe) — ch01; the submission-queue doorbell hands it to the controller's 8-16 parallel NAND channels — ch01; the FTL maps LBA to physical page, perhaps garbage-collecting a block — ch01; the NAND page is programmed, erasable only per 64-256-page block — ch01. Reclaim: kswapd scans the LRUs — clean pages drop free, dirty pages are forced down this same pipeline, and tmpfs pages go to swap, their only disk — ch05. The claim dies at the dirty page: nine arrows run below write()'s return, each owned by a chapter — the module in one picture (ch05).
