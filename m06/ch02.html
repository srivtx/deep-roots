Jetsam & the compressor

chapter 02 · ~14 min read · deep-roots learning program
The 30-second version. iOS has no disk swap, so when RAM runs short the system has two moves: compress cold dirty pages into a smaller store that stays in RAM (the kernel's compressor pager — CPU cycles for memory), and kill whole processes (Jetsam). Kills follow a fixed ladder of priority bands, bottom-up, largest footprint first within a band; processes with private caps ("highwater marks") die for exceeding those alone. The number that decides your fate is phys_footprint — dirty plus compressed pages — not RSS: compressing a 16 KB page to 4 KB frees 12 KB, and footprint, unlike RSS, keeps the surviving 4 KB on your bill. Your only warning is an advisory memory warning; the kill is a SIGKILL recorded in a JetsamEvent report.

No swap: deriving the two valves

Flash wears out. Consumer TLC NAND tolerates ~10^3 program/erase cycles per cell, and write amplification means a "4 KB write" moves more than 4 KB (controllers rewrite whole erase blocks). Desktop swap streams anonymous pages through that budget for hours; on a phone, that is hardware lifetime spent to avoid killing a background process.

Every page moved costs joules. A swap-out is a DRAM read plus a flash write; a swap-in is the reverse, both through the controller's queues. On battery, memory traffic is energy (module 01 ch22); compressing in RAM costs microseconds of CPU and no flash traffic.

Flash is slow where it hurts. A compressed page faults back in microseconds; a swapped page returns in milliseconds. Under pressure that difference is jank or a watchdog timeout.

So iOS has never had a swap file (iPhone OS 1 through iOS 17; macOS keeps one — different physics and budgets). Module 01 ch03's ledger: every physical page is file-backed (recoverable from a file) or anonymous (lives only in RAM). Three reclaim moves remain:

  1. Drop clean file-backed pages — nearly free; the next access re-faults the page from the file. Demand paging, working as designed.
  2. Compress cold dirty pages — keep the data, shrink its RAM cost.
  3. Evict a process — SIGKILL it; all its pages vanish at once.

Moves 1–2 are the first pressure valve: reclaim without a victim. Move 3 is the second valve — not reclamation but scheduling: deciding which process is cheapest to lose.

The compressor: a pager that trades CPU cycles for RAM

The compressor is a pager in the Mach VM sense (module 06 ch01's VM objects): a cold dirty page's data is encoded and parked in kernel-owned compressed segments still in RAM; the original page is freed. A later touch faults and decompresses it back — to the process, just a slower fault. No API, no signal, no callback.

Facts worth carrying (XNU source, macOS 14 / iOS 17 era):

  • Compression is per VM page, not a stream: each 16 KB page (4 KB on Intel Macs) is coded independently.
  • The scheme is hybrid and adaptive: WKdm where it compresses well, an LZ4 variant elsewhere (osfmk/vm/vm_compressor_algorithms.c). WKdm — a dictionary coder built for memory pages — has separate 4 KB and 16 KB code paths.
  • Real workloads shrink 2–3x (pages are mostly zeros, pointers, and repeats); the design arrived in iOS 7 / OS X 10.9 Mavericks (2013) and has not changed shape since. A thrashing store — pages compressed, faulted, compressed again — is a Jetsam trigger in itself.

The accounting puzzle: compression creates memory

The trap. Compress one 16 KB cold dirty page to 4 KB:

                         before                      after
  process's dirty set    16 KB (page P, resident)    0 KB
  compressor store       0 KB                        4 KB (P encoded, kernel-owned)
  physical RAM spent     16 KB                       4 KB     <- 12 KB CREATED
  process RSS            16 KB                       0 KB     <- "all 16 KB freed!"
  process phys_footprint 16 KB                       4 KB     <- the honest ledger

Three ledgers, three answers. Physical RAM: 12 KB came back — compression creates free memory. RSS: the 16 KB is gone — P no longer maps into the process's page tables, and the 4 KB copy sits in the kernel's pool, unattributed to any process's RSS; an RSS-only tool overstates the win by 4 KB per page. phys_footprint: keeps the 4 KB charged to the owning process — correct, since if that process dies, the bytes are freed too. The folded pages are still your pages; the number that predicts your death is phys_footprint, never RSS. Its recipe, from the ledgers kern_memorystatus.c:

phys_footprint = internal               (dirty anonymous pages, resident)
               + internal_compressed    (your pages, at compressed size)
               + purgeable_nonvolatile  (+ its compressed share)
               + alternate_accounting   (+ compressed; graphics-adjacent)
               + iokit_mapped           (mapped in by drivers)
               + page_table             (your own page tables)

Excluded: clean file-backed pages (droppable) and volatile purgeable pages (droppable by contract, below). And Apple Neural Engine buffers go to a separate neural_nofootprint ledger — ML workloads get a break the kill arithmetic never sees.

Jetsam: the band ladder

The kernel subsystem is memorystatus (bsd/kern/kern_memorystatus.c); "Jetsam" is the name on the reports. Every process sits in one priority band; under global pressure the kernel walks the ladder from the bottom and kills until pressure clears. Some processes also carry a personal cap — a highwater mark — and die for exceeding it regardless of global conditions. The whole policy in one picture (bands from bsd/sys/kern_memorystatus.h, modern XNU, macOS 14 / iOS 17 era; numbers get rescaled between releases, the order never is):

Global triggers. The kernel tracks a pressure level — normal, warn, urgent, critical — with free-page thresholds computed at boot from total RAM (why the same app survives on 6 GB and dies on 3 GB). Below threshold, the memorystatus thread kills from the lowest occupied band upward; within a band the bucket is sorted by footprint (JETSAM_SORT_FOOTPRINT), biggest hog first — except the idle/aging bands, age-ordered, oldest first. Compressor thrash and file-cache thrash (fc-thrashing) trigger it too.

Per-process triggers. Highwater-marked processes — app extensions, small daemons — are checked individually; one over its own mark dies with reason highwater even on an otherwise idle system. Foreground apps that cross their band's resident limit die with per-process-limit. Same hammer, different trigger arithmetic.

The kill is a SIGKILL (signal 9): no exception, no handler, no stack — and the kernel first snapshots every process with its page counts, so the report is a census, not just an obituary. The reason vocabulary (kern_memorystatus.c):

highwater               exceeded the process's own fixed mark
per-process-limit       crossed the resident limit for its band
vm-pageshortage         system-wide: not enough free pages
vm-compressor-thrashing the compressor is thrashing
vm-compressor-space-shortage  the compressed store itself is full
fc-thrashing            file cache thrashing
sustained-memory-pressure   pressure that lasts, not spikes
low-swap                macOS only: swap nearly exhausted
zone-map-exhaustion     kernel zone map full (module 06 ch01)
(also: jettisoned, vnode-limit, proc-thrashing, idle-exit)

The escape hatches user space actually gets

Memory warnings — the chance to yield before the hammer. When pressure crosses the warn level, user space learns through a dispatch primitive, not a signal:

/* C: watch the pressure level yourself, no UIKit required */
dispatch_source_t s = dispatch_source_create(
    DISPATCH_SOURCE_TYPE_MEMORYPRESSURE, 0,
    DISPATCH_MEMORYPRESSURE_WARN | DISPATCH_MEMORYPRESSURE_CRITICAL,
    dispatch_get_global_queue(QOS_CLASS_DEFAULT, 0));
dispatch_source_set_event_handler(s, ^{ /* drop caches, save state, NOW */ });
dispatch_resume(s);

UIKit repackages this as UIViewController.didReceiveMemoryWarning() (plus the classic applicationDidReceiveMemoryWarning()); UISceneDelegate, for all its life-cycle callbacks, has no memory-warning method of its own. Warnings are advisory — keep allocating and you die anyway, a suspended app cannot run any handler — and per-process-limit kills can arrive with no warning at all. Treat a warning as your last voluntary act, not a negotiation.

Purgeable memory — the three-tier economy. Module 01 ch03's two ledgers plus a contract: memory the app declares re-creatable.

tier                 examples                       footprint?  under pressure
clean (file-backed)  mmapped assets, code,              no      dropped, re-faulted
                     never-written file pages                    from the file
volatile purgeable   NSPurgeableData, ImageIO         no(*)     dropped silently;
                     caches, re-creatable buffers                app told on next
nonvolatile purgeable/ base maps you re-marked          yes     compressed or
dirty (anonymous)    Swift arrays, decoded images              killed with you
                     and other live objects

(*) kernel keeps volatile pages in 8 age groups (NUM_VOLATILE_GROUPS = 8,
    osfmk/vm/vm_purgeable.c) and drops the oldest group first.

The API is mach_vm_purgable_control (NSPurgeableData is the friendly wrapper): mark a range volatile when its contents are disposable; re-mark it nonvolatile later and the call tells you whether the kernel purged it meanwhile — regenerate or proceed. Volatile purgeable is the only dirty memory that costs you nothing: dirty by content, clean by promise.

os_proc_available_memory — how much runway do I have?

#include <libproc.h>
uint64_t runway = os_proc_available_memory();  /* bytes; iOS 13+ */

It answers "how much can phys_footprint grow before I hit my limit" — the number the Xcode gauge's dirty + compressed bar is drawn against. For scale: extensions get caps in the tens of MB; a foreground app on a 6 GB iPhone routinely lives at 3–5 GB (module 01 ch22); the com.apple.developer.kernel.increased-memory-limit entitlement (iOS 13.4+) lifts the cap on high-RAM devices.

What insiders know

  • The name map and the scale: reports say "Jetsam," kernel source says memorystatus (bsd/kern/kern_memorystatus.c). JETSAM_PRIORITY_INTERNAL = 999 puts kernel_task and launchd beyond the ladder; old blog posts quote a classic 0–21 scale, modern XNU uses 0–210 — the order is the stable API.
  • phys_footprint's exact ledger: internal + internal_compressed + purgeable_nonvolatile (+ compressed) + alternate_accounting (+ compressed) + iokit_mapped + page_table — plus Neural Engine buffers deliberately charged to neural_nofootprint: ANE traffic is invisible to the kill arithmetic.
  • Highwater kills are sysctl-gated: kern.memorystatus_highwater_enabled turns the per-process-cap path on or off. highwater is the fixed personal mark; per-process-limit is the band's resident limit.
  • JetsamEvent reports are bug_type 210 (app crashes are 309). They land as JetsamEvent-YYYY-MM-DD-HHMMSS.ips: Settings > Privacy > Analytics Data, Xcode > Devices and Simulators, or /var/mobile/Library/Logs/CrashReporter/ on a dev-unlocked phone. In production, MetricKit's MXAppExitMetric (iOS 14+) reports the exits without log-file access.
  • The report's useful fields: pageSize (16,384 on 16K devices — check before page math), largestProcess, and a processes array with name, uuid, rpages, lifetimeMax, states, frontmost per entry — and only the jettisoned process carries reason. Apple's rule: if the victim is not your app, your "crash" was not memory pressure.
  • Volatile purgeable is free footprint: 8 age groups, oldest dropped first; the compressor picks WKdm or LZ4 per page, with WKdm's 16 KB path existing because of module 06 ch01's 16 KB pages.

See it yourself

On any Mac (same XNU core; Apple Silicon Macs even share the 16 KB page size).

Footprint versus RSS, in your own shell — the whole accounting chapter in two commands:

$ vmmap --summary $$ | grep -i footprint
Physical footprint:          37.4M
Physical footprint (peak):   38.1M
$ ps -o rss= -p $$
    9184

$$ is the shell's pid. RSS says ~9 MB, footprint 37 MB; the gap is compressed pages, page tables, and IOKit mappings RSS refuses to attribute to you — and the Jetsam-relevant number is the bigger one. To watch the pressure machinery itself: sudo memory_pressure -l warn|critical forces a pressure level (watch Activity Monitor's memory-pressure graph go yellow/red and a memory-hungry app's compressed share climb — valve 1 at work; macOS then also swaps, which iOS never does, so kills are rarer), and /usr/bin/footprint <pid> prints the same accounting by category — the CLI twin of the Xcode gauge.

A JetsamEvent walkthrough (documented — no Mac needed). You need a real report from a device or Organizer; here is the reading discipline on a representative one (field names per Apple's Xcode docs and XNU's kern_memorystatus.c; Apple does not freeze this schema, so treat the JSON as version-dependent):

JetsamEvent-2024-05-21-161012.ips
header: { "bug_type":"210", "os_version":"iPhone OS 17.4 (21E219)", ... }
body:   { "pageSize":16384, "largestProcess":"SkyViewer", "processes":[
          { "name":"SkyViewer", "uuid":"8F...", "pid":531, "rpages":226000,
            "lifetimeMax":231000, "states":["frontmost"],
            "reason":"per-process-limit", "killed":... },
          { "name":"Music", "rpages":96000, ... },   <- no reason: not the victim
          ... one entry per process on the device ... ] }

Read it in this order: (1) bug_type 210 — a kill report, not a crash; no thread backtraces, by design. (2) pageSize × rpages = 16,384 × 226,000 ≈ 3.5 GB claimed. (3) Only the victim has reason — per-process-limit means the app crossed its own band limit while frontmost. (4) largestProcess confirms it was the biggest claim on the device. (5) lifetimeMax (peak footprint) says leak ratchet or one-shot spike. Had the reason been vm-pageshortage with the victim in band 30, the verdict flips: background roulette, not your bug — the difference between filing "OOM-killed by policy" and hunting a crash that never happened.

How to use this knowledge

  • Triage the bug report before blaming code. "App vanished with no stack while visible" is the textbook Jetsam signature. Pull Analytics Data, find JetsamEvent-*.ips, and check whether your bundle id is the entry with reason — if not, the crash is elsewhere; if yes, the reason string chooses your next step.
  • Let the reason string pick the fix. per-process-limit on a frontmost app → your footprint is the bug: mapped files instead of copied assets (module 01 ch03), volatile purgeable caches. highwater in an extension → the tens-of-MB cap is not negotiable; move the work and buffers back to the host app. vm-pageshortage while backgrounded → persist state before suspension so relaunch is cheap.
  • Size caches with the right number. Budget against os_proc_available_memory() and the dirty + compressed gauge, never RSS — an RSS-based limit looks safe precisely when it is not: compressed pages are invisible to RSS and still count against you.

Quiz

  1. Predict the numbers. An iOS app (16 KB pages) mmaps an 800 MB read-only asset and touches 20 MB; it allocates and touches 100 MB of anonymous memory. The kernel later compresses 40 MB of now-cold anonymous pages down to 10 MB. What are the app's RSS and phys_footprint, and which one predicts the kill?
  1. Concept. Compressing a 16 KB page to 4 KB "creates" 12 KB of free memory. What do RSS, physical RAM, and phys_footprint each report for that operation — and why does an RSS-only tool systematically overstate the benefit?
  1. Predict + connect. os_proc_available_memory() returns 500 MB. The code calls malloc(200 * 1024 * 1024) without touching it. What does the call return right after — and which two earlier chapters explain the mechanism and the structure behind that?

  1. Diagnosis. You implement didReceiveMemoryWarning to purge caches; the handler ran, and the app still died 400 ms later with reason per-process-limit. Give two distinct reasons the warning could not have saved it.
Answers
  1. Flash wear, joules, latency. (a) Consumer NAND tolerates ~10^3 program/erase cycles per cell — swap spends that budget continuously. (b) Every swapped page costs a storage read/write pair plus write amplification — energy on a battery. (c) Swapped pages fault back in milliseconds versus microseconds compressed — pressure becomes jank. Valves: compress cold dirty pages (reclamation — the data survives, only its size changes) and kill processes (scheduling — rank by importance, delete the cheapest, take all its pages).
  1. RSS 80 MB, phys_footprint 70 MB. RSS = 20 MB touched clean pages + 60 MB resident dirty; the compressed 40 MB left the page tables, and the copies sit in the kernel's pool, unattributed. Footprint = 60 MB dirty + 10 MB compressed = 70 MB; the untouched 780 MB never counted (touching costs, allocating does not), and the clean 20 MB is excluded (droppable, re-readable). Footprint is what the band limit checks; RSS predicts nothing.
  1. Physical RAM −12 KB (net free). RSS −16 KB (the page left the resident set; the 4 KB copy belongs to no process's RSS). phys_footprint −12 KB (internal 16 KB → internal_compressed 4 KB). An RSS-only tool reads "16 KB freed" and misses the 4 KB still owed — megabytes of invisible liability system-wide, exactly the debt the kill settles. Footprint keeps the copy charged because killing the process frees it.
  1. Still ~500 MB. malloc reserves address space; nothing is charged until a touch faults pages in — module 01 ch03's "malloc costs nothing; touching costs a fault." The enabler is module 06 ch01's VM map: regions backed lazily by VM objects, no physical page until first access. Touch all 200 MB and the answer drops ~200 MB — 200 MB of dirty internal pages.
  1. Not a crash — a policy kill. bug_type 210 with no thread backtraces is the Jetsam signature (crash reports are 309, with stacks). 226,000 × 16,384 ≈ 3.5 GB claimed by a frontmost app; per-process-limit means phys_footprint crossed the foreground band's resident limit — no system-wide shortage needed. Fix footprint: mapped files instead of decoded copies (clean, ~free), volatile purgeable caches — the gauge's dirty + compressed columns would have shown this coming weeks earlier.
  1. Extensions carry per-process highwater marks. The keyboard sits in foreground-support territory, but ~64 MB exceeded a personal cap checked independently of global pressure — highwater kills fire even on an idle system (kern.memorystatus_highwater_enabled gates the path). The host app's 1.5 GB is charged against a different, gigabyte-class limit. Caps assume extensions are small and short-lived; keep big buffers in the host — the extension's memory is its own bill.
  1. Both measure reclaim falling behind, differently. vm-pageshortage is a stock signal: free pages below the pressure level's threshold (set at boot from RAM size) — kills are level-driven. PSI is a flow signal: time tasks spend stalled in reclaim plus refault rates, so lmkd kills before free memory runs out. Both refuse victim-scoring because mobile is band policy first: the platform already ranked processes (oom_score_adj, the Jetsam ladder), and the kernel kills the worst band's fattest member; the desktop synthesizes a ranking at kill time (badness: size, age, privilege). Mobile ranks policy; desktop ranks victims.
  1. Two of several. (a) The warning and limit paths are independent: a per-process-limit kill is the ledger crossing the band limit, not the pressure level that produces warnings — it can arrive with no warning at all. (b) The kill is an asynchronous SIGKILL: once memorystatus decides, no user code — including a mid-purge handler — gets another instruction. (Credit: suspended apps cannot run handlers; warnings are advisory.) Make the app cheap to kill; you cannot make it unkillable.
