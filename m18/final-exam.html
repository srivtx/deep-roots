Module 18 final exam

every chapter · worked answers behind the fold

Twelve questions across the module's five chapters — three on entropy, three on the symmetric state machine, two on public-key bignums, two on TLS 1.3, two on the failure catalog. Closed book; every answer is worked, not stated — the derivations are the point. Scoring: 11+ = you own this module; 8-10 = solid, revisit the flagged chapters; <8 = the chapter quizzes are the study path.

Part I — entropy: randomness from physical memory (chapter 01)

  1. Diagnose. A TRNG datasheet claims "256 bits of entropy per boot" from 256 raw samples. Your measurement: the raw bit stream emits 1 with probability 0.6. (a) Compute the honest min-entropy per sample. (b) How many bits of real entropy do the 256 samples carry, and how many raw bits does an honest 256-bit seed require? (c) The vendor's whitepaper arrives at "249 bits" — which measure did they use, why is it the wrong one against an adversary's best single guess, and why will nobody notice the shortfall in the field?
  1. Predict. A CTR_DRBG (SP 800-90A) is seeded once with 256 honest bits: state = AES key + 128-bit counter, 2^19 bits per Generate call, reseed after 2^48 calls, and every call's update overwrites the old state. (a) How many bytes can flow between reseeds, and what does that number say about what the reseed interval actually is? (b) An attacker steals the live state on day 1,000: exactly what can she now predict, what can she never recover, and what puts an expiry date on her theft? (c) Chapter 05's catalog lists Debian, the clone fleet, and the wall-clock seed — which layer did not fail in any of them, and what does that say about where you audit?
  1. Predict (the number). A helpdesk mandates 8-character passwords from lowercase a–z, chosen "randomly" by users. (a) Bits of entropy if the choices really were uniform — and where does the number-sense table put that against a laptop at 10^9 guesses/s and a GPU rig at 10^12? (b) The same passwords behind a bcrypt-class KDF (attacker speed ÷10^5): recompute the GPU time. (c) Corpus-real human passwords carry 20–40 bits — how many random lowercase characters is that? (d) How many characters actually buy 80 bits, and what does a KDF buy instead of entropy?

Part II — the symmetric state machine (chapter 02)

  1. Derive (the birthday). A log-shipping fleet seals every record under one long-lived AES-GCM key. Each 96-bit nonce is device_id (32 bits, fixed per device) ‖ random (64 bits); all devices share the key. (a) One device ships 2^20 records: compute the nonce-collision probability. (b) At what record count does collision reach 50% per device — and what would the horizon be with a full random 96-bit nonce? (c) Recompute for a fleet of 1,024 devices each shipping 2^20 records. (d) One collision occurs: what does the attacker hold, and what is the one fix that repairs the structure rather than the instance?
  1. Predict (byte level). Two record prefixes were sealed under one CTR keystream — the nonce was reused. You know p1 = "GET " and hold c1 = DB F7 7E D7, c2 = FA DE 4B 90. Recover the keystream and p2, byte by byte. Then: what else does the recovered keystream buy, which single ciphertext byte would you flip to turn G into S in a live stream, and why does the same flip under GCM never reach the application?
  1. Diagnose. A vendor ships full-disk encryption as AES-GCM per 512-byte sector. Give three independent reasons that design is broken, with numbers: the per-sector framing cost, what a torn write on power loss does, and the per-key data cap against a 1 TiB disk. Then name the mode disks actually use, how it derives its "nonce," and why one flipped ciphertext bit garbling exactly one 16-byte block is deliberate.

Part III — bignums are the attack surface (chapter 03)

  1. Derive. On the lab's toy curve (y² = x³ + 2x + 2 over F_17, G = (5,1), order n = 19), a device signs H1 = 6 and H2 = 14 with the same nonce k and private key d, publishing (r, s1) = (10, 15) and (r, s2) = (10, 5). Recover k and d, showing every inverse mod 19. What single observable in the published signatures is the tell, and which two real incidents died of exactly this?
  1. Predict. Textbook modular exponentiation walks a 2,048-bit secret exponent: one squaring per bit, plus one multiply for every set bit. (a) Two keys with popcount 960 and 1,088: compute each operation count and the timing spread between them. (b) Per-signature network jitter dwarfs that spread — how does the attacker still win? (c) Curve25519's Montgomery ladder runs 251 iterations over the clamped key: compute its per-key operation counts and variance, and name what influence of the key bit survives. (d) For the RSA world, which cannot change algorithm shape, name the residual defense and its cost.

Part IV — TLS 1.3 as memory hygiene (chapter 04)

  1. Derive. A long-lived TLS 1.3 connection averages 1,400-byte records. (a) How many records exhaust the ~64 GiB GCM per-key budget, and where does the spec's rekey guidance of ~2^24.5 records sit relative to it? (b) Over that connection's life, which of the 12 nonce bytes ever change, and why? (c) KeyUpdate resets the sequence counter to zero — why is that the one permitted reuse of low counter values? (d) The nonce is never transmitted at all: what does refusing to put it on the wire buy, and which two failure modes does the implicit counter trade for it?
  1. Diagnose (the mapping). For each attack, name the single TLS 1.3 design decision that kills it and the memory structure where the kill lives: (a) BEAST (2011) — TLS 1.0 chained each record's CBC IV to the previous record's last ciphertext block, so the attacker saw the "IV" before choosing the next plaintext; (b) KRACK (2017) — WPA2's retransmitted handshake message 3 reinstalled the key and reset the packet counter to zero; (c) a MitM forging a TCP FIN mid-response so a payment client logs 3 KB of a 10 KB answer as success; (d) a passive recorder storing today's handshakes to decrypt later with a stolen server key. Then: which attack class did TLS 1.3 consciously leave alive, and who enforces the defense?

Part V — the failure catalog (chapter 05)

  1. Diagnose (byte level). A heartbeat request arrives: type 1, declared payload_length 0x4000, actual payload 4 bytes, 16 bytes of honest padding behind it. The response buffer is malloc(3 + payload + 16). (a) How many bytes does the memcpy read, and how many lie past the attacker's own data? (b) Why is the destination write in bounds while the read is the violation? (c) Why did redzone-style memory checkers not flag the original bug, and what does that imply for the incident response? (d) The one-line fix, the class name, and what leaked first from production heaps.
  1. Diagnose (timing level). A TLS 1.2 CBC server rejects injected records ~1–2 µs faster when the guessed padding is longer. (a) Walk the mechanism: what work shrinks with padding length, and why is "faster reject = better guess" an oracle? (b) The oracle answers one bit per query, at a few hundred queries per plaintext byte and ~2 million chosen records per session — how long at 250 queries/second, and how does the attacker beat LAN jitter to see microseconds at all? (c) OpenSSL's patch took three rounds — what was it, and why did it keep failing? (d) What did TLS 1.3 do instead, in one sentence that generalizes?
Answers

Part I

  1. (a) Min-entropy is the worst case: H∞ = −log2(p_max) = −log2(0.6) ≈ 0.737 bits/sample — an adversary always guessing "1" is right 60% of the time, and security prices your worst day. (b) 256 × 0.737 ≈ 189 bits — the datasheet overcounts by ~67. An honest 256-bit seed needs 256/0.737 ≈ 348 raw bits (44 bytes). (c) 249 = 256 × 0.971 is Shannon entropy (−0.6·log2 0.6 − 0.4·log2 0.4 ≈ 0.971 bits/sample): average surprise, which overprices a biased source ~3× at this bias — SP 800-90B's estimators shave for exactly this reason. Nobody notices in the field because a short seed produces keys that are syntactically perfect and pass every self-test — Debian's 15-bit seed ran ~20 months unnoticed; only precomputation ever sees it (ch01).
  1. (a) 2^48 calls × 2^16 B (2^19 bits = 64 KiB) = 2^64 B = 16 EiB between reseeds. The interval is bookkeeping, not a cliff: expansion is cheap because the seed was the whole budget. (b) She can predict every output until the next reseed — each call is AES-CTR keystream under the stolen key and counter. She can never recover pre-theft outputs: every call overwrote the old state — backtracking resistance (fails only if stale state lingers: ch04's zeroization discipline). Reseeding folds in fresh entropy, so the theft has an expiry date — prediction resistance. (c) The DRBG layer: in Debian the seed was deleted, in the clone fleet duplicated, in the wall-clock case public — the deterministic expansion never failed once in the catalog. Seeding code is a single point of silent failure: audit upstream (ch01, ch05).
  1. (a) 26^8 ≈ 2.09×10^11 ≈ 2^37.6 (8 × log2 26 = 8 × 4.70 = 37.6 bits). Laptop at 10^9/s: 209 s ≈ 3.5 minutes; GPU rig at 10^12/s: 0.2 s — the table's "32 bits = laptop seconds" row, one notch up. (b) 10^12/10^5 = 10^7 effective guesses/s → 2.09×10^11/10^7 ≈ 2.1×10^4 s ≈ 5.8 hours. (c) 20–40 bits = 4.3–8.5 random lowercase characters: 4–8 characters — humans are the entropy floor (and do not choose uniformly). (d) 80/4.70 ≈ 17 characters. The KDF adds zero entropy — it charges rent per guess (the ÷10^5), buying time, not margin: the honest fix is length, not iterations (ch01's number-sense table).

Part II

  1. (a) q = 2^20 draws with replacement from the 2^64 random suffix: pairs = q(q−1)/2 ≈ 2^39, each colliding with probability 2^−64, so P ≈ 2^39/2^64 = 2^−25 ≈ 3×10^−8. (b) 50% at q²/2^65 ≈ ½ → q ≈ 2^32.5 ≈ 6×10^9 records per device. A full random 96-bit nonce: 2^48.5 ≈ 4×10^14 — the fixed 32-bit prefix deletes 32 bits of birthday margin, a 65,536× collapse. (c) Device risks add linearly: 2^10 × 2^−25 = 2^−15 ≈ 3×10^−5 — a ~1-in-33,000 chance the fleet minted a reused (key, nonce). (d) The two-time pad: c1 ⊕ c2 = p1 ⊕ p2, and templated log records make p1 known, so p2 falls out verbatim. Worse — it is GCM: same (key, nonce) means same J0, so T1 ⊕ T2 = GHASH(C1) ⊕ GHASH(C2) is a polynomial in H whose roots hand over the authentication subkey — Joux's forbidden attack, forgery forever; rotate the key, not the nonce. The structural fix: move the device ID into the key — HKDF-Expand(master, device_id) gives every device its own key, restoring the full 96-bit nonce space and making cross-device collisions impossible; alternatively random 96-bit nonces under the 2^32-per-key cap (ch02).
  1. KS = c1 ⊕ p1: DB⊕47 = 9C, F7⊕45 = B2, 7E⊕54 = 2A, D7⊕20 = F7 → KS = 9C B2 2A F7. Then p2 = c2 ⊕ KS: FA⊕9C = 66 'f', DE⊕B2 = 6C 'l', 4B⊕2A = 61 'a', 90⊕F7 = 67 'g' → p2 = "flag" (equivalently, c1 ⊕ c2 = 21 29 35 47 = p1 ⊕ p2 — the two-time pad's signature). The keystream decrypts any record sealed under that (key, nonce) and enables bit-aligned forgery: XOR 0x14 into ciphertext byte 0 — 0x47 'G' ⊕ 0x14 = 0x53 'S' — and the receiver parses "SET" with no error: unauthenticated CTR is a bit-editing machine. Under GCM the flip changes a GHASH input, the tag no longer matches, and the record dies at InvalidTag before the application sees one byte (ch02).
  1. (a) Framing: every sector would carry a 16-byte tag plus a stored 12-byte nonce — 28/512 = 5.5% of the disk. (b) Torn write: power loss mid-write leaves sector and tag inconsistent; the next read fails authentication and the mount refuses — the disk bricks itself on a power glitch: per-sector authentication is structurally impossible, not merely expensive. (c) The GCM cap: 2^36 B ≈ 64 GiB per key — a 1 TiB disk needs 2^40/2^36 = 16 HKDF-derived keys; and the nonce discipline (persisted per-sector counters) is precisely the crash/rollback state that reuses nonces exactly. Disks use XTS: the tweak is the sector number, encrypted under a second key and advanced by powers of α per block — position derives the "nonce," so there is no state to reset, replay, or collide. One flipped ciphertext bit garbles exactly one 16-byte plaintext block — deliberate damage localization, honest caveat: not truly wide-block (ch02's insiders).

Part III

  1. Identical r across two different messages is the tell: r is the x-coordinate of k·G, so the same r means the same nonce k. Subtract the signing equations: (s1 − s2)·k = H1 − H2, so 10·k ≡ 6 − 14 ≡ −8 ≡ 11 (mod 19); 10^−1 ≡ 2 (10·2 = 20 ≡ 1), so k = 11·2 = 22 ≡ 3. Then d = (s1·k − H1)/r: 15·3 − 6 = 39 ≡ 1 (mod 19); r^−1 = 2, so d = 1·2 = 2. Two inverses — the private key, the same algebra that signed the PS3's firmware (fixed hardcoded k, 2010) and emptied Android/Bitcoin wallets (PRNG state collisions, 2013), scaled to 256-bit n. The fix is RFC 6979's deterministic k = HMAC(d, H(m)): deterministic beats random when randomness is the attack surface (ch03).
  1. (a) Key A: 2,048 + 960 = 3,008 operations; key B: 2,048 + 1,088 = 3,136 — a ~4.3% timing spread purely from popcount (mean 1,024, σ ≈ 22.6; these keys sit at ±2.8σ). (b) One signature's jitter is 100× the signal, but averaging N measurements shrinks noise ~√N: thousands of timed signatures turn 4% into certainty — the same statistics dudect industrializes as a Welch t-test — and windowed exponentiation then leaks which bits — the table index is secret bits choosing cache lines (Tromer: an adjacent VM, ~100 ms). (c) The ladder: 251 iterations, each exactly 1 doubling + 1 differential addition + 1 conditional swap — 251 doubles, 251 adds, 251 cswaps, every key; variance zero by construction. The surviving key influence is which register plays which role, via a branchless swap: nothing reaches branch direction, operand size, or memory address (and 2^255 − 19 reduces with shifts and ×19 — no comparisons). (d) Blinding: m′ = m·r^e mod n, sign m′, divide by r (r^(ed) ≡ r by the chapter's own congruence); cost ~20% plus a precomputed r^e, r^−1 pool — the timing distribution the attacker measures becomes one over r, not d (ch03).

Part IV

  1. (a) 2^36 B / 1,400 B ≈ 4.9×10^7 ≈ 2^25.5 records exhaust the budget; the ~2^24.5 ≈ 2.4×10^7 rekey guidance sits at half that — margin, not coincidence. (b) The nonce is static_iv ⊕ seq with the 64-bit counter right-aligned in 12 bytes: bytes 0–3 XOR zeros forever, and below 2^32 records the counter's high 4 bytes are zero too — so only the last 4 of 12 nonce bytes ever change; the other 8 stay frozen at static_iv. (c) KeyUpdate installs a fresh traffic key when it resets the counter — every (key, nonce) pair after the reset is new: low values recur, pairs never; key and counter state must travel together (KRACK violates exactly this law). (d) Transmitting the nonce would spend 12 bytes per record and expose the one value GCM dies on; hiding it deletes both — at the price of implicit state that must agree on both ends. Failure mode one, desync (sender restarts at 0, receiver expects 5): every tag fails, bad_record_mac — the polite death. Failure mode two, both ends restarting at 0 under the same key: identical (key, nonce) pairs — the two-time pad, and for GCM H itself recoverable, forgery forever (ch04; ch02).
  1. (a) AEAD-only, nonce implicit. The 1.0 "IV" was the previous record's last ciphertext block — visible before the attacker chose the next plaintext, so she could aim the first block and test the guess (BEAST's chosen-plaintext cookie recovery; an IV must be unpredictable, not merely unique). TLS 1.3 has no CBC, no padding, and no transmitted IV at all: nonce = static_iv ⊕ sequence number, rebuilt from a per-direction counter the attacker never sees before encryption — there is nothing left to predict. (b) Counters reset only with new keys. KRACK's shape is state replay: retransmission logic reinstalled the key and zeroed the packet counter, replaying (key, nonce) pairs. 1.3's KeyUpdate law resets the counter to 0 only alongside a fresh traffic key — low values recur, pairs never. (c) close_notify. Each side must send the encrypted alert (record type 21) before closing its write half, and the library must surface EOF-without-close_notify as an error, not EOF — OpenSSL 3.0's SSL_R_UNEXPECTED_EOF_WHILE_READING exists for this — a forged FIN is byte-perfect at the TCP layer: truncation is detected one layer above the layer that can be truncated. (d) Ephemeral DHE plus the erasure schedule. TLS 1.2's RSA key transport encrypted the premaster to the long-term key — record once, steal the key later (Heartbleed was the theft event), decrypt history. 1.3 deletes the mechanism (DHE only) and erases on schedule: the DH private key the moment the handshake secret exists, handshake traffic secrets after both Finished messages, application secrets at close — past captures stay dead because the bytes are. Consciously left alive: 0-RTT replay — early data rides a PSK-only traffic secret an active MitM can re-send verbatim; no protocol layer can know which of your requests is safe to run twice: the application enforces it (idempotent GET yes, POST never) (ch04).

Part V

  1. (a) The memcpy copies the declared 16,384 bytes starting at the payload pointer: 4 bytes the attacker actually sent, then 16 honest padding bytes still inside the record's chunk, then 16,364 bytes past the message — chunk slack, the next chunk's header, its payload. Total beyond the attacker's own data: 16,380. (b) The response buffer was sized 3 + 16,384 + 16 = 16,403 bytes before the copy, so the write fits its allocation; the violation is entirely the source read — a declared length never checked against the record that actually arrived. A heap over-read — the complement of use-after-free, touching bytes the program never logically owned; it needs no write primitive because the read's content is echoed back verbatim. (c) In OpenSSL the source read stayed inside one malloc'd 16 KiB record buffer — stale recycled bytes, not out-of-allocation bytes — so redzone checkers saw a legal access; what leaked was whose stale neighbors sat there (record buffers recycle through thousands of connections): session keys first — a mid-connection heap is full of them — then private keys, passwords, cookies. Hence: rotate everything, not patch-and-move-on. (d) The fix is one bounds check: if (1 + 2 + payload + 16 > n) return 0;. The class: TLV framing bugs — a state machine must never trust a length field it doesn't own. One line of code versus re-issuing certificates for ~17% of the secure web (ch05).
  1. (a) MAC-then-encrypt: the receiver decrypts, strips k padding bytes, then HMACs what remains — so a valid padding guess of length k shortens the MAC's input by k bytes (k ∈ 1..16), and the hash work shrinks ~1–2 µs per step. The reject is faster exactly when the padding guess is more correct: timing is a function of work, and the work varied with a secret-derived value — a padding oracle that decrypts ciphertext without the key. (b) 2×10^6 records / 250 per second = 8,000 s ≈ 2.2 hours. Microseconds against LAN jitter: repeat each query thousands of times and average — noise shrinks ~√N while the µs bias does not; the same statistical amplification dudect formalizes as a t-test. Vaudenay's ~few hundred queries per plaintext byte compound into the session. (c) The patch: constant-time padding handling — HMACs over dummy maximal lengths, every padding treated alike, no branching on the pad value. It took three rounds because the first two were found still leaky in 2013: constant time decays under refactor — it is measured (dudect, ctgrind), never claimed — re-run after every refactor. (d) TLS 1.3 removed the grammar: AEAD only, the tag computed over the ciphertext and verified before one byte of plaintext is parsed, so the "bad padding" branch cannot exist. Remove the class of bug, don't fix each instance (ch05; ch04).
