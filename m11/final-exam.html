Module 11 final exam

every chapter · worked answers behind the fold

Twelve questions across all five chapters. Closed book. Every answer is worked, not just stated — the derivations are the point. Scoring: 11+ = you own this module; 8-10 = solid, revisit the flagged chapters; <8 = the chapter quizzes below each chapter are the study path.

Part I — the contract and the read-optimal tree (chapters 01-02)

  1. State precisely what fsync() guarantees on returning 0, two things it does not guarantee for a 16 KB page written just before it, and the hardware feature that makes two otherwise-identical SSDs differ ~10x in fsync latency.
  1. A 40 GB table on an HDD doing ~100 random IOPS and ~200 MB/s sequential; 8 KB pages. Compute the full-scan time for sequential layout, for rows scattered on random pages, and the ratio; name the module 01 chapter it re-states.
  1. A B+ tree: 16 KiB pages, 16-byte interior entries, leaves of 500 entries, guaranteed minimum fill 1/2. (a) Guaranteed maximum page reads for a point lookup at 10^9 entries? (b) The primary key becomes a 36-byte random UUID — recompute the worst case, and name two costs beyond depth. (c) A balanced BST over the same keys: how many reads, and where does the gap physically come from?
  1. InnoDB, users(id BIGINT PRIMARY KEY, email, name, city), secondary index on (email); query SELECT name FROM users WHERE email = ?. Count the full tree descents, say what changes with an index on (email, name), and what breaks if secondary leaves stored physical record pointers instead of primary keys.

Part II — the write-optimal tree (chapter 03)

  1. Derive the bloom-filter false-positive rate at 12 bits per key (optimal k), then at a halved 5 bits per key. With ~12 worst-case probes per absent-key point read, how many wasted data-block reads does each budget buy? Show the derivation, not a remembered number.
  1. Write latency is fine for hours, then p99 sawtooths to 800 ms; during spikes num-files-at-level0 = 9, actual-delayed-write-rate nonzero, estimate-pending-compaction-bytes growing. Diagnose to mechanism; name two knobs and what each trades.
  1. Map the LSM onto module 01 ch12's generational heap (memtable, flush, L0/L1, deep levels, compaction, dead versions, tombstone), name one structural difference, and state what each ancestry — LSM vs the B-tree's free-list allocator — predicts about write-amp and space-amp.

Part III — durability (chapter 04)

  1. Flushed LSN = 8,000; dirty pages needing eviction now: P7 (page LSN 6,200), P12 (7,850), P21 (8,500), P30 (11,900). Which flush now, which are blocked, in what order? Twenty transactions reach their commit point during the forced log flush — what do they get, at what cost?
  1. InnoDB on ext4, 4 KB sectors, 16 KB pages, doublewrite ON. Power dies mid page-flush; after reboot, page 47's checksum fails. Which write tore, where is the intact copy, what does recovery do, and why is doublewrite folklore-disabled on ZFS?
  1. Module 10 ch04's card-table barrier and the WAL rule are the same discipline. Name the two players and the ordering each enforces, and the one-word difference in what each protects.

Part IV — concurrency (chapter 05)

  1. Read view: low_water = 100, high_water = 121, active[] = {100, 118}. Chain, newest → oldest: trx 118 (current, clustered index), trx 105, trx 100, trx 80 (undo). (a) Which version does the reader return, by which steps? (b) T118 commits 1 ms later; the same REPEATABLE READ transaction repeats the query — what changes? (c) A different session, READ COMMITTED, queries after T118's commit with T100 still running — what does it return? (d) Did T118's writer ever wait for this reader?
  1. PostgreSQL: disk climbs though the workload is 95% DELETEs; one idle in transaction pid with a 4-day-old xact_start; a hot table's scans are 3x slower; autovacuum never catches up. Give the mechanism chain from pid to symptom, and the fix.
Answers
  1. On return 0, the fd's dirty data — and enough metadata to reach it — is on non-volatile media, pushed through the drive's own volatile cache. Not guaranteed: atomicity — the disk commits at sector granularity, so the 16 KB page is four 4 KB sector writes and any subset can land (a torn page); and scope — fsync covers only its own fd. The ~10x: power-loss-protection capacitors. An enterprise SSD acknowledges the flush from capacitor-backed cache (~0.1 ms); a consumer drive must program NAND first (~1-10 ms). Commit latency ≈ fsync latency (ch04), so capacitors are a commit-path feature in a storage spec sheet (ch01).
  1. 40 GB / 8 KB ≈ 5.2M pages. Sequential: 200 MB/s ≈ 25,000 pages/s → ~205 s. Random: 100 pages/s → ~52,000 s ≈ 14.5 hours. Ratio ≈ 250x: random access priced per trip, sequential by bandwidth — module 01 ch02's "latency is not throughput," the pressure behind clustered leaves (ch02), sequential flushes (ch03), the append-only log (ch04). On SSDs the gap survives at ~10-60x via FTL erase-block GC, not the seek arm (ch01).
  1. (a) Worst-case fanout F = (16,384 / 16) × 1/2 = 512; capacity(h) = F^(h-1) × 500. h = 3 → 512² × 500 ≈ 1.3 × 10^8, too small; h = 4 → 512³ × 500 ≈ 6.7 × 10^10. Guaranteed maximum: 4 page reads — guarantees use minimum fill; the tree is never shallower than its emptiest pages promise. (b) The interior entry becomes ~44 bytes → F ≈ 186; h = 4 capacity ≈ 186³ × 500 ≈ 3.2 × 10^9 — still 4 reads, but headroom collapses from ~67x to ~3x, and past ~3 billion rows the tree gains a level. Beyond depth: the fat PK is duplicated into every secondary leaf (shrinking their fanout), and random keys force 50-50 splits — ~69% fill, ~40% more bytes, no rightmost hot page (ch02). (c) log2(10^9) ≈ 30: comparisons are free in-cache next to a ~100 µs page read, one per level — the logarithm's base is the whole design (ch02).
  1. (a) Two full descents: the email tree (its leaf holds (email, id)), then the clustered PK tree for the row. (b) (email, name) carries every queried column — name, email, and the PK riding free in every secondary leaf — so one descent; EXPLAIN shows Using index. (c) Rows physically move — splits, merges, compaction — so record pointers would dangle and need cascading repair on every structure modification; the PK is a stable logical locator. Tax: PK bytes in every secondary leaf, plus a second descent per non-covered lookup (ch02).
  1. After n insertions a bit is still 0 with probability (1 − 1/m)^{kn} ≈ e^{−kn/m}; a non-member passes only if all k probes hit set bits: FP ≈ (1 − e^{−kn/m})^k, minimized at k = ln 2 · m/n, where the filter is half full and FP ≈ 0.6185^(m/n). 12 bits/key: k = 0.693 × 12 ≈ 8, FP ≈ 0.6185^12 ≈ 0.3%. 5 bits/key: k ≈ 3, FP ≈ 0.6185^5 ≈ 9% — the rate is exponential in bits, so halving the budget is a ~30x worse FP. An absent key worst-case probes ~12 structures (2 memtables + 4 overlapping L0 files + 6 levels): ~12 × 0.003 ≈ 0.04 wasted data-block reads at 12 bits vs ~12 × 0.09 ≈ 1.1 at 5 — point reads degrade from ~1-2 I/Os to ~2-3 for 0.9 bytes/key (ch03).
  1. Leveled-compaction debt: ingest outran merge throughput; L0 piled past the slow trigger (8 files — per-write ~ms delays; stop at 12) while the debt metric grows. Writers are throttled deliberately — backpressure bounding read-amp, since each overlapping L0 run is another probe on every future read; the spikes are merges chewing debt, not lock contention. Knobs: max_background_compactions/compaction threads (merge throughput for CPU and I/O); bigger write_buffer_size (fewer, larger flushes); raised L0 triggers (write latency for read-amp); or universal/tiered compaction — write-amp 1-5x instead of 20-30x, paid at ~2x space-amp (ch03).
  1. Memtable = eden/nursery; flush = minor-GC evacuation; L0/L1 = survivor spaces; ×10 level sizing = tenuring; deeper levels = old generation; compaction = mark-compact; dead versions = unreachable objects; tombstone = a deferred free(). Difference: liveness by recency (newest version wins — no tracing from roots), and the pause lands on writers while readers never stop. Ancestry predicts: the LSM is the copying/compacting collector — write-amp 20-30x (live data copied level by level), space-amp ~1.1x; the B-tree is the free-list allocator — update in place, low write-amp, perpetual fragmentation (69%-fill pages, splits, vacuum) as its space tax (ch03, ch02, module 01 ch12).
  1. P7 and P12 flush immediately: page_LSN ≤ 8,000, their history is already durable. P21 and P30 are blocked by the WAL rule (page_LSN ≤ flushed_LSN): first force the log past 11,900 — one fsync unblocks both — then write the pages in either order. The rule keeps no page ahead of its explanation: had P30 landed before records 8,001-11,900 were durable, recovery would face a page with no surviving description — effect without record. The 20 committers attach to that in-flight fsync — group commit: 20 durable commits for one flush, each waiting about one fsync epoch; the log force was obligatory anyway, so they ride it nearly free (ch04).
  1. The tear hit the second write — from doublewrite scratch (2 MB, 128 × 16 KB) to the tree. The first write (batch → scratch) was fsynced complete, so an intact copy sits in scratch. Recovery copies the clean image into place, then redo continues from that page's LSN. The log alone cannot fix it: physiological redo needs a valid starting page, and a half-old, half-new page never legally existed — ch01's sector atomicity, caught by the checksum. On ZFS/btrfs the filesystem never overwrites in place, so mid-page tearing cannot happen and doublewrite's 2x write-amp is pure waste there (ch04, ch01).
  1. Card-table barrier: the mutating thread and the GC; ordering is card dirtying before the pointer store becomes visible, so the collector never reasons from a stale heap. WAL rule: the page flusher and crash recovery; ordering is log durability before page durability (page_LSN ≤ flushed_LSN), so the recoverer never meets a page whose changes have no record. Same discipline — a happens-before edge forced at a store, protecting an asynchronous observer from effect-without-record. One word: the barrier protects visibility, the WAL rule durability (ch04, module 10 ch04).
  1. (a) trx 118: step 3 — in active[] → invisible; follow the roll pointer. trx 105: below high_water, above low_water, not in active[] → step 4: visible. The reader returns trx 105's version; trx 100 and 80 are never reached — 100's active status is a red herring. (b) Nothing: T118 committed after the snapshot was fixed, so it stays in the frozen active[] for this transaction's life — the frozen list is what "repeatable" means. (c) READ COMMITTED takes a fresh view per statement: active[] = {100}, so trx 118 passes step 4 — the RC reader returns trx 118's version. Same row, same instant, different answer: the isolation level is snapshot lifetime. (d) No — the writer's new version lives in bytes the reader never touches; the read view, not a lock, decided (ch05).
  1. The idle transaction holds the oldest snapshot, and the oldest snapshot is the collector's root boundary: vacuum cannot purge past it (long transaction = GC root, vacuum = collector, bloat = fragmentation). A DELETE is a write — it stamps t_xmax and frees no bytes — so four days of dead tuples and dead index entries pile up behind the frozen boundary; history the root pins never truncates. Heap and indexes bloat, scans read 3x the pages, disk climbs, queries slow and pin more — the death spiral. Fix: pg_terminate_backend(pid), then idle_in_transaction_session_timeout to prevent recurrence. InnoDB's version of the same alarm: a climbing History list length (ch05).
