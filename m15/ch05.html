module 15 · one machine, many owners

The cloud as a computer

chapter 05 · ~23 min read · deep-roots learning program
The 30-second version. Stop drawing the cloud as a cloud. A datacenter is one machine — the warehouse-scale computer — whose backplane is a spine-leaf fabric (honestly ~3:1 oversubscribed at the rack, never the brochure's "non-blocking"), whose northbridge is the rack's top-of-rack switch, and whose kernel is the fleet scheduler: your instance is a job in a globally distributed epoll loop. The economics that pay for it all are oversubscription — 2 vCPUs are one SMT2 core at ~1.2-1.3× throughput, memory is sold 1.05-1.5× over the DIMMs, and network and disk are token buckets: what you're guaranteed is the refill rate, not the pipe. At the bottom sit two deliberate bets on running strangers' code: Firecracker (~50 kLOC of Rust, a whole VM per function, 125 ms boot, <5 MB overhead) and gVisor (the kernel's ~400-syscall surface re-implemented in userspace at 2-10× syscall cost). The chapter closes the whole program with one table: who owns the bytes at every layer, process to DIMM, each row's fault domain, blast radius, and escape cost.

One machine, ~10^5 cores: the datacenter as a motherboard

The phrase is Barroso and Hölzle's ("The Datacenter as a Computer", 2009), and it is meant literally. Once a machine has ~10^5 servers, the interesting computer is the building, and every part you learned in modules 01-14 has a correspondent one level up:

one computerthe datacenter

northbridgetop-of-rack (ToR) switch

power railthe PDU feeding the rack
process tablethe fleet scheduler's placement tables

The fabric deserves its honest arithmetic. A spine-leaf network has two tiers: every ToR (leaf) uplinks to every spine switch, and ECMP hashing spreads flows across the spines — no spanning tree, many parallel paths. Now count one ToR: 48 downlink ports at 25 GbE = 1,200 GbE of demand from the rack; 4 uplinks at 100 GbE = 400 GbE of escape. That is 3:1 oversubscription, and it is the industry's common compromise (roughly 2:1 for HPC tiers, 5:1 for cheap tiers — name the tier before quoting a number). The brochure's "non-blocking" is technically true and practically empty: it promises the spine fabric's internal links never oversubscribe; it says nothing about the 3:1 at the leaf your traffic must cross. Two servers in the same rack talk at full line rate through the ToR's switching chip; the same two servers across racks enter the ratio.

The scheduler is where the analogy earns its keep. Module 01 ch04 derived the process as a data structure the kernel walks; the fleet scheduler walks the same idea at 10^6 scale: a table of machine structs (capacity, health, labels) and a table of job structs (requests, constraints, priority), and a bin-packing loop that settles on the order of one placement decision per second. Google's published example is Borg (EuroSys 2015 paper): cells of roughly 10^4-10^5 machines. Bin-packing is memory allocation one level up — the same fragmentation-versus-density trade the kernel's page allocator makes, at gigabyte granularity instead of 4 KiB. Your instance is a job in a globally distributed epoll loop: schedulable, preemptible, migratable — a struct in someone else's table.

The analogy breaks in exactly two places, and both are the chapter's subject. First, time constants: the kernel balances load in microseconds and preempts a CPU in nanoseconds; the fleet scheduler decides in milliseconds-to-seconds, and "moving a job" (live migration, ch03) takes seconds-to-minutes. Second, failure is routine: a motherboard does not lose a DIMM bank per day, but a fleet of 10^5 machines loses whole servers at a low-single-digit percent per year (published fleet-reliability figures land around 1-4%/year; the exact number varies by study). The control plane treats machine death the way the kernel treats a page fault: drain, reschedule, carry on — forever. Everything between those two breaks is oversubscription, the next section.

Selling the same core twice: CPU and memory

The cloud's economic engine is one bet, applied to every resource: customers don't use what they buy at the same instant. Here is the bet per resource, with the ratios that make it honest.

CPU. On the mainstream EC2-style families, 1 vCPU is 1 SMT thread — 2 vCPUs are one physical core (check the instance page; the .metal and some dedicated families sell whole cores). SMT2's measured gain on server mixes is 1.2-1.3× one thread's throughput, not 2×: the second thread only fills bubbles in the shared execution ports. Worse, the sharing is visible: a memory-bound sibling streaming through the core's L1/L2 evicts a compute-bound sibling's working set, costing it 20-40% — module 01 ch02's shared-cache lesson biting one level up. This is the noisy neighbor inside the core: your thread ran its fully-entitled time, so steal-time accounting sees nothing. Above the core, the oversubscription is visible: pack 10 instances × 2 vCPU (20 vCPUs of demand) onto an 8-core SMT2 host (16 threads of supply) and each saturated vCPU runs 16/20 = 80% of the time — the missing 20% is charged to the guest as steal time, the st column of top/vmstat (ch01's accounting: a vCPU that was runnable but ran someone else). st is the noisy neighbor's fingerprint without identity — by design, the guest cannot see whose. Inside the host, the contested share is cpu.weight (cgroup v2: default 100, range 1-10000): under contention, weights 200 vs 100 split the contended CPU 2:1.

Memory. Providers sell 1.05-1.5× the installed DIMMs (policy, not physics; the number varies by provider and tier). The tools are ch02's, at fleet scale. Ballooning — the garbage collector for VMs — is where fleets earn their folklore: the memory pressure storm. A node under pressure asks its guests for memory back; the balloon drivers force the guest kernels to reclaim; the guests evict their own page caches (their fast path) and may swap inside themselves (ch02's double-paging disaster); latency spikes across every guest on the node before anything officially "fails". The runbook rule writes itself: bursty memory tenants and oversubscribed nodes don't mix — keep them apart in the scheduler, the same way ch02 kept vNUMA guests node-local. The cheaper lever comes first: MCR, the memory compression ratio — compress cold guest pages in the host kernel at a typical 2:1-3:1 (workload-dependent) and nobody inside the guest feels it. Compress, then balloon, never host swap. And one policy locks in: cross-VM page sharing (KSM) is disabled on untrusted tiers, because dedup puts two tenants' pages on one physical cache line — module 02 ch07's FLUSH+RELOAD channel, priced in someone else's secrets.

The token bucket economy: network and disk

Network and disk oversubscription share one data structure: the token bucket. Refill rate r, burst size b: you may spend b tokens instantly, and you sustain exactly r. Every "up to 10 Gbps" instance is a bucket at the hypervisor's virtio path; every burstable volume is a bucket of IO credits. The law that decodes the billing curve: your sustained rate is the refill rate.

The network arithmetic: a host with a 100 GbE NIC happily sells "up to 10 Gbps" to 10 VMs — 100 GbE of burst demand, fine while uncorrelated, and the hypervisor's buckets cap each VM regardless. But the host's own uplink into the ToR is section 1's 3:1. On AWS's Nitro machines the bucket isn't even in software: dedicated cards own the network and volume IO (the endpoint of ch03's implementation ladder — SR-IOV-family offload), which is how an EBS volume appears in the guest as a local NVMe device (nvme-Amazon_Elastic_Block_Store) while actually being a network RPC to a replicated storage fleet.

Disk is where the token bucket has a name on the spec sheet. A gp2 volume: baseline 3 IOPS per GiB (refill), burst cap 3,000 IOPS, initial credit balance 5,400,000 I/O credits (AWS's EBS docs; gp3, from December 2020, replaced the mechanic with a flat 3,000 IOPS baseline). A 100 GiB volume sustains 300 IOPS forever, bursts to 3,000 while credits last. Run the law: an application averaging 600 IOPS drains the full bucket at 600 - 300 = 300 credits/s → 5,400,000 / 300 = 18,000 s = 5 hours, then the volume clamps to 300 IOPS and the queue depth explodes. The CPU version is the t2.small: 1 vCPU, 12 CPU-credits/hour (1 credit = 1 vCPU-minute) = 20% of one core sustained, full core in bursts — same law, different bucket.

The last trade is placement: local NVMe vs network volume. Local NVMe answers in ~20-50 µs (module 14 ch01's numbers) and dies with the host; a network volume is replicated within the zone, snapshot-able, and ~100-200 µs on its fast path (io-class volumes, small queue depth — gp-class runs ~1 ms; measure with fio before quoting). Those two latencies are the entire stateless-service architecture: local disk as cache, replicated volume as truth, host death as a cache flush nobody mourns.

Firecracker: a VM per function

Lambda's brief, stated as a threat model: run mutually distrusting strangers' code, thousands of concurrent sandboxes per host, cold start budget 125 ms, isolation stronger than a container. Chapter 04 disqualified the container: one kernel 0-day escapes it, and Lambda's tenants are exactly the people who find kernel 0-days. Full QEMU fails the other constraints: 1.5-2 MLOC of C (as of the 2020s) of device-emulation attack surface, and ~100-200 MB of per-VM overhead (ch04's table) — 2,000 VMs × 150 MB = 300 GB of host RAM spent before a single customer workload runs.

Firecracker (AWS, 2018, open-sourced; Rust) is the cuts, listed:

  • The device model: no PCIe bus topology, no USB, no display. The guest sees virtio-net, virtio-block, a serial console (Firecracker 1.x adds vsock; 1.3+ a balloon) on virtio-mmio — a surface you can audit in an afternoon, where QEMU's machine re-creates an entire chipset.
  • The VMM itself: jailed (chroot-style, minimal fds) behind a seccomp allowlist.
  • No live migration — a Lambda sandbox lives minutes; instead, snapshot/restore: the microVM's memory and device state frozen to files, resumed on any host in the fleet. It is the fork trick scaled: read-only shared memory, copied on first write — module 01 ch03's copy-on-write semantics implemented by the VMM instead of the kernel. Restore lands in single-digit-to-tens of milliseconds for Lambda-sized VMs (grows with memory size).
  • The numbers: 125 ms from launch to workload (the Firecracker paper's own figure), <5 MB of VMM memory overhead per microVM (paper's figure), thousands of microVMs per host — 2,000 × 5 MB = 10 GB, density QEMU cannot reach at 2,000 × 150 MB.

The honest summary: Firecracker is less general than QEMU on purpose. It cannot boot a 2003 Windows ISO, pass through a USB webcam, or migrate. What it does is shrink the code that parses untrusted input from ~1.5-2 MLOC to ~30-50 kLOC — roughly 40× less attack surface — and that ratio, not any benchmark, is the design argument. Fargate and Fly.io adopted it for the same arithmetic.

gVisor: the kernel moves to userspace

The opposite pole from Firecracker: don't add a boundary below the kernel — a second page table, a guest mode — but shrink the boundary the kernel exposes. gVisor (Google, open-sourced 2018; the runsc OCI runtime) inserts the sentry, a Go program that stands where the kernel stands in a container's life: the container's syscalls are intercepted (via ptrace, a KVM platform, or sytrap since 2022) and implemented — the file system (a helper "gofer" process brokers real host files), the network stack (netstack, a userspace TCP/IP), and the memory management, all in Go.

The cost is paid per syscall, and it is honest: the intercept plus the sentry's own work makes syscall-bound paths 2-10× slower (gVisor's documentation concedes syscall-heavy workloads suffer). The tell is frequency, not volume: an accept()-storm benchmark (a busy web server's connection churn) hurts, a streaming upload mostly doesn't. The payoff is the same argument as ch04's "attack surface is an API surface", extended: the container's ~400 syscalls never reach the host kernel — the kernel sees only the sentry's own narrow set, on the order of tens of syscalls — and a bug in the sentry's re-implementations crashes a sandbox instead of rooting the host. The line item ch04 never had: the sentry is the kernel — VFS, netstack, and the app's page tables (an mm managed from userspace), all Go code a bug cannot turn into ring 0.

Both poles answer the same threat model with opposite geometry: Firecracker gives each workload its own untrusted kernel behind a ~50 kLOC wall (you pay a kernel per workload); gVisor replaces the kernel's surface with Go (you pay 2-10× on each syscall). Strangers' code needs one of them; trusted teams' containers need neither.

Who owns the bytes: the whole program in one table

The program began with module 01's question — who owns which bytes, and who moves them — asked about a single process. The cloud answers it five times, once per layer. Build the table a row at a time, each row one chapter of this module:

  • Process: owns its virtual address space; the physical bytes are the kernel's (module 01 ch03's allocator, ch04's process struct).
  • Container: owns namespace views, cgroup limits, and the overlayfs upperdir; the bytes are host pages charged to its cgroup (ch04).
  • VM: owns its guest-physical memory — a QEMU mmap translated by EPT and charged to the host's accounting (ch02); its kernel is its own.
  • Hypervisor: KVM inside the host kernel (ch01's architecture: the host kernel is the hypervisor) — the real page tables, the host's slab and heap.
  • Host: the DIMMs and ECC — module 01 ch01's bit flips stop here; the last line of defense is hardware.

Read the table as a trade: each layer up buys a stronger wall (escape cost rises from "one 0-day" to "physical access") by accepting a bigger fault domain (from "one process" to "the whole node"). The cloud is what happens when someone decides that trade at fleet scale, ~10^5 times, with a scheduler sweeping up the failures.

What insiders know

  • st is per-vCPU wall-clock, not host load: 20% means your vCPU was ready and unpaid-for 20% of the time. It never names the neighbor — a fingerprint without identity, which is why cloud support asks for the time pattern (business hours = the neighbor's schedule, not yours).
  • The SMT sibling is the noisy neighbor you cannot prove: your vCPU ran 100% of its entitled time and still lost 20-40% to cache eviction. No column in top shows it; only co-tenancy placement does.
  • cpu.weight (default 100, range 1-10000) is the contested share — 200 vs 100 splits contended CPU 2:1 — while cpu.max is a hard ceiling. Weight is an argument, quota is a wall.
  • The burst law: sustained = refill. t2.small: 12 credits/hour = 20% of a core forever. gp2: 3 IOPS/GiB forever, 3,000 while the 5.4M-credit bucket lasts. Both clamp at exactly the refill rate, and both cliffs look like "the disk got slow at hour 5", not like an error.
  • The lever order under memory pressure: compress (MCR 2:1-3:1, the guest never feels it), balloon second (the guest thrashes), host swap never (the latency cliff of ch02).
  • Firecracker's design argument is one division: ~1.5-2 MLOC / ~30-50 kLOC ≈ 40× less untrusted-input-parsing code than QEMU. Quote the ratio, not a benchmark.
  • gVisor pain predictor: syscall frequency, not bytes. 2-10× per syscall — an accept() storm hurts, a bulk upload doesn't.
  • The interview trap: "the container is slow, the host is 65% idle" — look at cpu.stat's nr_throttled before anything else. The host's idleness and the container's quota are independent variables (ch04's 10 ms-per-100 ms stall class).

On a laptop or bare metal, st is 0.0 forever — the column exists for the day you are the guest. On a busy shared VM during business hours, 0-20% is routine. The point is knowing which of the five columns is the loud neighbor's fingerprint.

3. Your own quota, your own stall (cgroup v2: stat -fc %T /sys/fs/cgroup prints cgroup2fs):

sudo systemd-run --unit=cap50 -p CPUQuota=50% python3 -c 'x=0
while True: x += 1'
sudo systemd-run --unit=nocap python3 -c 'x=0
while True: x += 1'
top -d 2      # press 1: nocap's python ~100% of a core, cap50's pinned ~50%
cat /sys/fs/cgroup/system.slice/cap50.service/cpu.max    # 50000 100000
cat /sys/fs/cgroup/system.slice/cap50.service/cpu.stat
sudo systemctl stop cap50.service nocap.service

CPUQuota=50% compiles to cpu.max = 50000 100000: 50 ms of CPU per 100 ms period — half a core, hard ceiling. While saturated, cpu.stat reads like a confession:

usage_usec 5123400
nr_periods 10240
nr_throttled 10231        <- throttled in nearly every period
throttled_usec 5119000    <- roughly the missing half

That is ch05's container-vs-instance lesson in one file: the instance is slow, the host is idle, and the owner of the problem is your own cgroup.

4. The pid-namespace arithmetic (needs unprivileged user namespaces; Debian/Ubuntu default on):

unshare -Ufp sh -c 'echo "pid as I see it: $$"; grep NSpid /proc/self/status'
# pid as I see it: 1
# NSpid: 31245 1                <- host pid first, innermost pid last

unshare -Urmpf --mount-proc sh -c 'grep NSpid /proc/self/status'
# NSpid: 1                      <- NSpid lists from the procfs mount's
#                                  namespace down; remounting /proc in the
#                                  new ns collapses the line

The gap between those two outputs is the pid namespace: one process, two counters, and the list's length tells you how many namespaces deep you are.

5. The burst-credit curve from a real spec sheet (gp2's numbers, EBS docs):

size_gib, app_iops = 100, 600
baseline = 3 * size_gib          # 300 IOPS: the refill = sustained rate
credits  = 5_400_000             # full bucket at t=0
drain    = app_iops - baseline   # 300 credits/s net burn
print(credits / drain / 3600)    # 5.0 h until the clamp to 300

Change app_iops to 700 and re-run: 3.75 h. The bucket empties in inverse proportion to how far above the refill rate you live.

How to use this knowledge

  • Capacity planning before the incident: size for the refill rate, treat burst as a battery. The 5-hour gp2 arithmetic is a spreadsheet cell, and the answer "we clamp at 14:00 daily" is knowable the week before launch.
  • The first 60 seconds of "it's slow": us/sy/id/wa/st, then cpu.stat — five numbers with five different owners (your app, your kernel, idle, your disk, someone else's scheduler), and the diagnosis is which number moved.
  • Choosing an isolation pole: strangers' code → Firecracker-class microVMs or gVisor, priced in LOC of attack surface (~50 kLOC vs ~2 MLOC) and syscall latency (2-10×); trusted teams → containers, priced in kernel 0-days. The table's last column is the decision.
  • Justifying stateless architecture with latency: local NVMe at ~20-50 µs as cache, replicated volumes at ~100 µs-1 ms as truth — the numbers, not fashion, are the argument, and the host-death-as-cache-flush failure mode is the design's feature.
  • The interview answer: "why does Lambda boot a VM per call?" — 125 ms boot, <5 MB overhead, ~40× less attack surface than QEMU, thousands per host. Four numbers, each doing one sentence of work.

Answers
  1. Time constants and failure as routine. Break one: the kernel preempts in nanoseconds and load-balances in microseconds; the fleet scheduler places on the order of one decision per second and moves a job by live migration in seconds-to-minutes — a ~10^6× gap between the levels' clock speeds. Break two: a motherboard treats a DIMM loss as a catastrophe; the fleet treats a machine loss as a page fault — at ~1-4%/year per machine across ~10^5 machines, that is roughly one dead "DIMM bank" every few hours, drained and rescheduled in seconds. Both breaks are exactly why oversubscription (the next section) is even possible: the slack between the levels is the product being sold.
  1. Because the shared resources are shared. SMT2's two threads share the core's execution ports and caches: the second thread fills bubbles, worth 1.2-1.3× on typical server mixes, not 2× — the ports were never idle twice over. The sibling interference (20-40%) is the cache half of that sharing: a memory-bound stream evicts the L1/L2 working set (module 01 ch02), and st stays 0 because steal-time accounting measures only scheduling — the hypervisor gave the vCPU its fully entitled time; the contention happened inside the core. Memory oversubscription stops at 1.05-1.5× (not 2×) because the reclaim tools are violent: the balloon forces guests to evict their own page caches and double-page, and the memory-pressure storm (scenario 2 of the lab) turns one extra gigabyte sold into every guest's P99. The ratio is set by how much reclaim pain the provider will sell before customers notice.
  1. (a) 60-65%. 16 vCPUs = 8 physical cores × SMT2. A fully saturated workload gets 8 × 1.2-1.3 ≈ 9.6-10.4 core-equivalents of throughput — call it 60-65% of 16 dedicated cores. The 2-vCPU-per-core mapping is a 35-40% discount the spec page never prints. In the first command, /proc is still the original mount (its namespace is the host's), so the line shows the host pid then the new-namespace pid — the two-counters-one-process truth of ch04. In the second, --mount-proc remounts /proc inside the new pid namespace, so the list starts there and collapses to the single innermost pid. When the host asks for memory back, the balloon driver allocates inside the guest, and the guest's kernel must free pages now: its cheapest victim is the page cache — the very thing making its workloads fast. Every read that was a cache hit becomes a disk read; if the guest is really tight, it swaps inside itself to satisfy the balloon, and each of those pages then faults back through the host — ch02's double-paging spiral, now multiplied across 10 guests. The result is exactly the observed tripled P99 with no failed health check. The cheaper first lever is host-side compression — MCR ~2:1-3:1, cold guest pages squeezed in the host kernel while guests run unaware. The runbook rule: bursty-memory tenants and oversubscribed nodes don't mix — schedule them apart before the balloon ever wakes.
